    Subsection 1.2.5 The Binomial Series

    The series we obtained for the inverse tangent function in the previous problem is actually also valid for \(x=1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.20.

    Use this to obtain
    \begin{equation*} \pi =4\left(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{\frac{{\left(-1\right)}^n}{2n+1}}\right)=\frac{4}{1}-\frac{4}{3}+\frac{4}{5}-\frac{4}{7}+\frac{4}{9}-\dots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Now be honest, would you have ever thought that the right-hand side would converge to \(\pi \text{?}\)
    🔗
    The above series for the arctangent was known to James Gregory (1638-1675) before the invention of calculus. Leibniz independently discovered the series by examining the area of a circle and communicated his ideas for determining areas via series to Newton through an intermediary, John Collins, who also was in contact with Gregory and communicated his ideas to Newton.
    🔗
    It is true that the value of \(\pi \) can be approximated to any degree of accuracy by summing a finite number of terms of the series
    \begin{equation*} 4\left(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{\frac{{\left(-1\right)}^n}{2n+1}}\right)=\frac{4}{1}-\frac{4}{3}+\frac{4}{5}-\frac{4}{7}+\frac{4}{9}-\dots \end{equation*}
    but, unfortunately, this converges very slowly and it takes adding an enormous amount of terms to get any accuracy. For example, we have the approximation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 4\left(\sum^{1000}_{n=0}{\frac{{\left(-1\right)}^n}{2n+1}}\right)=\frac{4}{1}-\frac{4}{3}+\frac{4}{5}-\frac{4}{7}+\frac{4}{9}-\dots +\frac{1}{2001}\approx 3.142591654 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    which is only accurate to two decimal places. While Newton praised the elegance of Leibniz in his methods, he set about to find a series approximation for \(\pi \) which would converge faster. We will use modern terminology to streamline Newton’s ideas. First notice that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\pi }{4}=\int^1_{x=0}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \dx{x} \end{equation*}
    as this integral gives the area of one quarter of the unit circle. The trick now is to find a power series which represents \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\text{.}\) We will start with something you may or may not have seen in precalculus: the Binomial Theorem.
    🔗
    It is pretty straightforward to expand \({\left(1+x\right)}^2=1+2x+x^2\) but let’s do it carefully to recognize the pattern.
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^2=\left(1+x\right)\left(1+x\right)=1\left(1+x\right)+x\left(1+x\right)=\left(1+x\right)+\left(x+x^2\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+\left(x+x\right)+x^2=1+2x+x^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Proceeding in a similar manner, we get
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^3=\left(1+x\right){\left(1+x\right)}^2=\left(1+x\right)\left(1+2x+x^2\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1\left(1+2x+x^2\right)+x\left(1+2x+x^2\right)=\left(1+2x+x^2\right)+\left(x+2x^2+x^3\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+\left(2+1\right)x+\left(1+2\right)x^2+x^3=1+3x+3x^2+x^3 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^4=\left(1+x\right)\left(1+3x+3x^2+x^3\right)=1\left(1+3x+3x^2+x^3\right)+x\left(1+3x+3x^2+x^3\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =\left(1+3x+3x^2+x^3\right)+\left(x+3x^2+3x^3+x^4\right)=1+\left(3+1\right)x+\left(3+3\right)x^2+\left(1+3\right)x^3+x^4 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+4x+6x^2+4x^3+{1x}^4 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    At this point could you recognize that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^5=1+\left(4+1\right)x+\left(6+4\right)x^2+\left(4+6\right)x^3+\left(1+4\right)x^4+x^5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} =1+5x+10x^2+10x^3+5x^4+{1x}^5 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^6=1+\left(5+1\right)x+\left(10+5\right)x^2+\left(10+10\right)x^3+\left(5+10\right)x^4+\left(1+5\right)x^5+x^6 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+6x+15x^2+20x^3+15x^4+6x^5+x^6 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Keeping track of the exponents is straightforward enough. To keep track of the coefficients, mathematicians devised the following
    🔗
    This is known as Pascal’s Triangle in honor of Blaise Pascal (1623-1662) though it was known to Persian, Hindu, and Chinese mathematicians some 500-600 years before Pascal. Pascal called this triangle an Arithmetical Triangle and utilized it to solve problems dealing with probability. The name fits: notice that each entry is obtained by adding the two entries directly above it as we did in our expansions. As such, this provides the coefficients in our expansion. For example,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^7=1+7x+21x^2+35x^3+35x^4+21x^5+7x^6+x^7 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If one had patience, they could use the triangle to determine the expansion of \({\left(1+x\right)}^{20}\text{.}\) Fortunately, there is a quicker way to do this.
    🔗
    \begin{align*} {\left(1+x\right)}^{20}=1\amp{}+\frac{20}{1}x +\frac{20\cdot 19}{2\cdot 1}x^2+\frac{20\cdot 19\cdot 18}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^3\\ \amp{} +\frac{20\cdot 19\cdot 18\cdot 17}{4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^4+\dots \\ \amp{} +\frac{20\cdot 19\cdot 18\cdot \dots \cdot 1}{20\cdot \dots \cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^{20} \end{align*}
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.22.

    Based on the formula we stated above, we should have
    \begin{align*} \left(1+x\right)^7=1+\frac{7}{1}x+\amp{}\frac{7\cdot 6}{2\cdot 1}x^2\\ \amp{} +\frac{7\cdot 6\cdot 5}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^3\\ \amp{} +\frac{7\cdot 6\cdot 5\cdot 4}{4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^4\\ \amp{} +\frac{7\cdot 6\cdot 5\cdot 4\cdot 3}{5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^5\\ \amp{} +\frac{7\cdot 6\cdot 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2}{6\cdot 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^6\\ \amp{} +\frac{7\cdot 6\cdot 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}{7\cdot 6\cdot 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1}x^7 \end{align*}
    Compute these coefficients to verify that we get the same result as we did using Pascal’s Triangle. If you cancel out terms before you multiply, you can do these without a calculator.
    🔗
    🔗
    The general form of this expansion is
    \begin{align*} {\left(1+x\right)}^N=1+\frac{N}{1!}x+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)}{2!}\amp{}x^2\\ +\amp{}\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)}{3!}x^3\\ \amp{} + \dots +\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1}{N!}x^N \end{align*}
    and this result is known as the Binomial Theorem which holds for any nonnegative integer \(N\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.23.

    In most books, you will see the binomial theorem written as
    \begin{equation*} {\left(a+b\right)}^N=a^Nb^0+\frac{N}{1!}a^{N-1}b^1+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)}{2!}a^{N-2}b^2+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)}{3!}{a^{N-3}b}^3+\dots +\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1}{N!}{a^0b}^N \end{equation*}
    You can see that our formulation is a special case of this where \(a=1\) and \(b=x\text{.}\) It turns out that our formulation can be used to obtain the general formula as well, so they are logically equivalent. To see this, rewrite \({\left(a+b\right)}^N=a^N{\left(1+\frac{b}{a}\right)}^N\text{.}\) Let \(x=\frac{b}{a}\) in
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^N=1+\frac{N}{1!}x+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)}{2!}x^2+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)}{3!}x^3+\dots +\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1}{N!}x^N \end{equation*}
    to obtain the general formula
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(a+b\right)}^N=a^Nb^0+\frac{N}{1!}a^{N-1}b^1+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)}{2!}a^{N-2}b^2+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)}{3!}{a^{N-3}b}^3+\dots +\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1}{N!}{a^0b}^N \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    In summation notation, our version of the binomial theorem looks like this.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^N=1+\sum^N_{n=1}{\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\dots \left(N-\left(n-1\right)\right)}{n!}x^n} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.24.

    Write out some of the terms in the above summation to verify that it agrees with what we wrote as the Binomial Theorem. Of special note is what the terms look like when \(n=1\) and when \(n=N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Our formulation of the Binomial Theorem is a finite sum, but if we followed the pattern, we could have written this as a power series.
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^N=1+\frac{N}{1!}x+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)}{2!}x^2+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)}{3!}x^3+\dots +\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1}{N!}x^N+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1\cdot 0}{\left(N+1\right)!}x^{N+1}+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\cdot \dots \cdot 2\cdot 1\cdot 0\cdot \left(-1\right)}{\left(N+2\right)!}x^{N+2}+\dots \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\dots \left(N-\left(n-1\right)\right)}{n!}x^n} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice that after the \(x^N\) term, all of the coefficients would be zero. The reason we did this is to be as audacious as Newton and try this for values of \(N\) that are not nonnegative integers. For example, suppose we try this for \(N=-1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.25.

    Substitute \(N=-1\) into the formula
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^N=1+\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)\left(N-3\right)\dots \left(N-\left(n-1\right)\right)}{n!}x^n} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+\frac{N}{1!}x+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)}{2!}x^2+\frac{N\left(N-1\right)\left(N-2\right)}{3!}x^3+\dots \end{equation*}
    to obtain a series representation for \({\left(1+x\right)}^{-1}\text{.}\) How does this compare to the result you got from substituting \(-x\) into the geometric series?
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.26.

    In the last section, you differentiated the geometric series to obtain a series for \({\left(1-x\right)}^{-k}\text{.}\) Show that you get the same result by substituting \(-x\) into the binomial series with \(N=-k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice that unlike the case where \(N\) was a nonnegative integer, these series do not end. Also notice that this says that there is a limitation to this binomial series as this only works for \(-1\lt x\lt 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Newton was so audacious that he proposed that this binomial series representation works for non-integer values of \(N\) as well. For example, if \(N\) is the rational number \(p/q\) where \(p,q\) are integers with \(q\neq 0\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^{\frac{p}{q}}=1+\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{\frac{p}{q}\left(\frac{p}{q}-1\right)\left(\frac{p}{q}-2\right)\left(\frac{p}{q}-3\right)\dots \left(\frac{p}{q}-\left(n-1\right)\right)}{n!}x^n} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} =1+\frac{\frac{p}{q}}{1!}x+\frac{\frac{p}{q}\left(\frac{p}{q}-1\right)}{2!}x^2+\frac{\frac{p}{q}\left(\frac{p}{q}-1\right)\left(\frac{p}{q}-2\right)}{3!}x^3+\dots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Again, notice that unless \(\frac{p}{q}\) is a nonnegative integer, the coefficients in this sum will never be zero and it truly will be an infinite series.
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.27.

    In a letter to Henry Oldenburg dated June 13, 1676, Newton described his binomial series and included the example
    \begin{equation*} \sqrt{c^2+x^2}=c+\frac{x^2}{2c}-\frac{x^4}{8c^3}+\frac{x^6}{16c^5}-\frac{5x^8}{128c^7}+\frac{7x^{10}}{256c^9}+\dots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Rewrite \(\sqrt{c^2+x^2}=c{\left(1+{\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)}^2\right)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\) and substitute \(\frac{x}{c}\) into the binomial series with \(N=\frac{1}{2}\) to obtain Newton’s result.
    🔗
    🔗
    It should be noted that Newton did not prove that his general binomial series was correct but convinced himself that the pattern worked for rational powers beyond nonnegative integers. We did not prove that this is correct either, though we have some evidence that it is as we saw with negative integer powers.
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.28.

    If the series
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^2=1+\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)}{2!}x^2+\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\right)}{3!}x^3+\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-3\right)}{4!}x^4+\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-3\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-4\right)}{5!}x^5+\dots =1+\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{1}{8}x^2+\frac{1}{16}x^3-\frac{5}{128}x^4+\frac{7}{256}x^5+\dots \end{equation*}
    is correct, then multiplying the series by itself should result in \(1-x\text{.}\) We can apply the “box method” of multiplying polynomials below to see if this how this starts to play out.
    🔗
    \(\times\) \(1\) \(\frac{1}{2}x\) \(-\frac{1}{8}x^2\) \(\frac{1}{16}x^3\) \(-\frac{5}{128}x^4\) \(\dots \)
    \(1\) \(1\) \(\frac{1}{2}x\)
    \(\frac{1}{2}x\) \(\frac{1}{2}x\) \(\frac{1}{4}x^2\)
    \(\frac{-1}{8}x^2\)
    \(\frac{1}{16}x^3\)
    \(\frac{-5}{128}x^4\)
    \(\vdots \)
    Fill in the rest of this multiplication table and use it to show that if you multiply the series
    \begin{equation*} 1+\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{1}{8}x^2+\frac{1}{16}x^3-\frac{5}{128}x^4+\frac{7}{256}x^5+\dots \end{equation*}
    by itself, then you get
    \begin{equation*} 1+x+0x^2+0x^3+0x^4+\dots \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Of course, this is still not proof that the series is correct (unless we compute all of the coefficients), but we are getting a good amount of evidence that Newton’s binomial series is correct. We also know from our work with the geometric series that it seems to converge for \(-1\lt x\lt 1\text{.}\) In fact, the series
    \begin{align*} \left(1+x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=1+\frac{1}{2}x+\amp{}\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)}{2!}x^2\\ \amp{}+\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\right)}{3!}x^3\\ \amp{} +\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-3\right)}{4!}x^4\\ \amp{} +\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-3\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-4\right)}{5!}x^5+\dots \end{align*}
    so that
    \begin{equation} \left(1+x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} =1+\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{1}{8}x^2+\frac{1}{16}x^3-\frac{5}{128}x^4+\frac{7}{256}x^5+\dots\tag{1.2.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Also converges for \(x=\pm 1\text{,}\) but this is beyond this course. [For a proof of this, you can refer to the open-source textbook How We Got From There to Here: A Story of Real Analysis, by Boman and Rogers.
    🔗
    Recall that Newton intended to provide a series representation for \(\frac{\pi }{4}=\int^1_{x=0}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \dx{x}\) to find a series which converges to \(\pi \) faster than Gregory’s series.
    🔗

    Problem 1.2.5.0.29.

    Use the power series (1.2.2) to obtain the series
    \begin{align*} \frac{\pi }{4}\amp{}=\int^1_{x=0}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \dx{x}\\ \amp{}=1+\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\dots \left(\frac{1}{2}-\left(n-1\right)\right)}{n!}}\left(\frac{{\left(-1\right)}^n}{2n+1}\right)\\ \amp{}=1-\frac{1}{6}-\frac{1}{40}-\frac{1}{112}-\frac{5}{1152}-\dots \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Adding the first 50 terms of this gives us the approximation
    \begin{equation*} \pi \approx 1+\sum^{49}_{n=1}{\frac{\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}-1\right)\dots \left(\frac{1}{2}-\left(n-1\right)\right)}{n!}}\left(\frac{{\left(-1\right)}^n}{2n+1}\right)\approx 3.14267 \end{equation*}
    which provides the same sort of accuracy that Gregory’s series took 1000 terms to get. Utilizing the binomial series, Newton was able to obtain series that converge even faster. We can also use this series to obtain some more power series representations and faster converging approximations for \(\pi \text{.}\)

    We do want to warn you that we have been very flippant in our use of power series so far. Everything we have done is correct, but it is much more nuanced than we may have led you to believe. For example, consider the general binomial series
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} {\left(1+x\right)}^{\alpha }=1+\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{(\alpha \left(\alpha -1\right)\left(\alpha -2\right)\dots \frac{\alpha -\left(n-1\right)}{n!}} x^n \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The values of \(x\) for which this is valid depend on the value of \(\alpha \text{.}\) For example, if \(\alpha \) is a nonnegative integer then this works for \(x\) as the sum is finite, and it is really the binomial theorem. For other \(\alpha \text{,}\) we have that this holds for \(x\in [-1,1]\) when \(\alpha >0\text{,}\) for \(x\in (-1,1]\) when \(-1 \lt \alpha \lt 0\text{,}\) and for \(x\in (-1,1)\) when \(\alpha \le -1\text{.}\) We will be getting into some of these nuances later in the theory part, but for now let’s just start with the following question: Which functions can be represented by a power series at all?
