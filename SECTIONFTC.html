    Section 7.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

    As we mentioned before, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus was known to both Newton and Leibniz and some of their predecessors. Leibniz’ differential notation (along with Fourier’s definite integral notation) makes this result seem very natural.
    🔗
    If \(\frac{dF}{\dx{x}}=f\) , then \(dF=f\dx{x}\text{,}\) so that
    \begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{f \dx{x}}=\int^b_{x=a}{dF}=F\left(b\right)-F(a) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    In fact, to Leibniz, this was so natural that he published in a 1693 paper entitled ``Supplementum Geometriae Dimensoriae\textit{ ...}" (Supplement on geometric measurement {\dots}). The fact that he called this a supplement seems to belie it being fundamental. In fact, the first rigorous proof of this was given by Augustin Cauchy in 1823, and even he did not refer to it as the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, though he and others certainly knew of its importance. How it got the name “Fundamental Theorem” is unknown to the authors, but we will provide what is essentially Cauchy’s proof. First, we need a preliminary result known as the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.2.0.1. The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals.

    Suppose \(f(x)\) is continuous on \([a,b]\text{.}\) Then there is a value \(c\) between \(a\) and \(b\) with
    \begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}=f(c)(b-a) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Before we prove this, if you anticipate that the integral represents the antiderivative of \(f\) and write this as
    \begin{equation*} {\left.\left(\int{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}\right)\right|}^b_{x=a}=f(c)(b-a) \end{equation*}
    Then this resembles the regular Mean Value Theorem we saw in the theory section of the differential calculus part of this book. The thing that makes this different is that we don’t yet know that the integral (as a limit of Riemann sums) is the antiderivative yet. This is what Cauchy proved (which we will do after we prove this result).
    🔗
    To see why the MVT for Integrals is true, notice first that if \(a=b\text{,}\) then setting \(c=a=b\) will work as both sides of the equation are automatically zero. So, we might as well assume that \(a\lt{}b\text{.}\) Since \(f(x)\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) then by the Extreme Value Theorem, it attains its maximum and minimum somewhere on \([a,b]\text{.}\) Label these \(M\) and \(m\) respectively. Thus, for all \(x\in [a,b]\text{.}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} m\le f\left(x\right)\le M \end{equation*}
    So that
    \begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{m \dx{x}}\le \int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)dx}\le \int^b_{x=a}{M\dx{x}} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} m\left(b-a\right)\le \int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}\le M\left(b-a\right) \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} m\le \frac{\int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}}{b-a}\le M \end{equation*}
    Since \(\frac{\int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}}{b-a}\) is a value between the minimum and maximum of the continuous function \(f(x)\text{,}\) then by the Intermediate Value Theorem, there is a \(c\in [a,b]\) with
    \begin{equation*} f\left(c\right)=\frac{\int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}}{b-a} \end{equation*}
    Multiplying by \(b-a\) gives us the MVT for Integrals.
    🔗

    Problem 7.2.0.2.

    In the statement of the MVT for Integrals, we assumed \(a\le b\) and considered the integral \(\int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}\text{.}\) Show it also works for \(\int^a_{x=b}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}\text{.}\) That is, show that there is a \(c\in [a,b]\) with
    \begin{equation*} \int^a_{x=b}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}=f(c)(a-b) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    Notice that \(\int^a_{x=b}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}=-\int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)dx}\text{.}\) By what we have shown, there is a \(c\in [a,b]\) with
    \begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{f\left(x\right)\dx{x}}=f\left(c\right)\left(b-a\right) \end{equation*}
    You should be able to finish it from here.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Armed with this we can state and prove Cauchy’s version of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
    🔗

    Theorem 7.2.0.3. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Cauchy).

    Suppose \(f(x)\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and define \(G(x)\) on \([a,b]\) by
    \begin{equation*} G\left(x\right)=\int^x_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}} \end{equation*}
    Then \(G(x)\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and is differentiable on \((a,b)\) with \(G^\prime \left(x\right)=f(x)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Before we prove this, notice that this says basically that a continuous function is guaranteed to have an antiderivative. Also notice that we first needed to rigorously define our Riemann integral and showed that it existed for a continuous function to ensure that \(G(x)\) is well defined.
    🔗
    To prove this, we will first show that \(G^\prime \left(x\right)=f(x)\) on \((a,b)\text{.}\) We begin with letting \(x\in (a,b)\) and consider
    \begin{align*} G^\prime \left(x\right)\amp{}=\limit{h}{0}{\frac{G\left(x+h\right)-G\left(x\right)}{h} }\\ \amp{}=\limit{h}{0}{\frac{\left(\int^{x+h}_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}-\int^x_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}\right)}{h} }\\ \amp{}=\limit{h}{0}{\frac{\int^{x+h}_{t=x}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}}{h} } \end{align*}
    🔗
    We want to show that this limit exists and is \(f^\prime (x)\text{.}\) To see this, notice that by the MVT for Integrals, there is \(c\) between \(x\) and \(x+h\) with
    \begin{equation*} \int^{x+h}_{t=x}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}=f\left(c\right)\left(x+h-x\right) = f\left(c\right)\cdot h\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Thus
    \begin{equation*} G^\prime \left(x\right)={\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}_{h\to 0} \frac{\int^{x+h}_{t=x}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}}{h} }={\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}_{h\to 0} \frac{f\left(c\right)\cdot h}{h} }={\mathop{\mathrm{lim}}_{h\to 0} f\left(c\right) } \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(c\) is between \(x\) and \(x+h\text{,}\) then as \(h\to 0\text{,}\) \(c\to x\text{,}\) so by the continuity of \(f\) at \(x\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} G^\prime \left(x\right)=f\left(x\right) \end{equation*}
    This also establishes that \(G(x)\) is continuous on \((a,b)\text{.}\) To get the continuity at \(a, \)we want to show that
    \begin{equation*} \rlimit{x}{a}{(G(x)-G(a))} \end{equation*}
    To see this, we have
    \begin{equation*} \rlimit{x}{a}{(G(x)-G(a))} = \rlimit{x}{a}{\left(\int^x_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}\right)} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Again, if we use the MVT for integrals on this, there is \(c\) between \(a\) and \(x\) with
    \begin{equation*} \int^x_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}=f(c)(x-a)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus,
    \begin{equation*} \rlimit{x}{a}{(G(x)-G(a))}= \rlimit{x}{a}{\left(f(c)(x-a)\right) }=f(a)\cdot 0=0 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Problem 7.2.0.4.

    Use a similar argument to show that
    \begin{equation*} \llimit{x}{b}{(G(x)-G(b))}=0 \end{equation*}
    so that \(G(x)\) is continuous at \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    So, we’ve shown that a continuous function has an antiderivative, but this doesn’t quite look like Leibniz’ version of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Leibniz’ version is a result of the following problem.
    🔗

    Problem 7.2.0.5.

    Suppose \(f(x)\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(F\left(x\right)\) is any antiderivative of \(f(x)\) which is continuous on \([a,b]\text{.}\) Show that
    \begin{equation*} \int^b_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}=F\left(b\right)-F(a) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    We have two antiderivatives of \(f(x)\) which are continuous on \([a,b]\text{:}\) \(F(x)\) and \(\int^x_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}\text{.}\) These must differ by a constant, so
    \begin{equation*} F\left(x\right)=\int^x_{t=a}{f\left(t\right)\dx{t}}+C \end{equation*}
    What must this constant be?
