    Section 20.1 Historical Precursors of Definite Integration

    Subsection 20.1.1 Calculus In Antiquity: The Method of Exhaustion

    It appears to me that if one wants to make progress in mathematics, one should study the masters and not the pupils.
    🔗
    ―Niels Henrik Abel (1802—1829)
    🔗
    It is a little startling to learn that the fundamental ideas underlying Calculus have been in place literally for millennia. It is even more startling to learn that these fundamentals existed not just as abstract theories but in a form which was useful (and used) for actual computation (though the calculations could be very difficult) from the beginning.
    🔗
    Eudoxus of Cnidus (408BC–355BC) is considered by some to be the original inventor of Calculus—predating Newton and Leibniz by nearly two thousand years—because he invented what has been called the Method of Exhaustion. Speaking very loosely Eudoxus’ idea was to “fill up,” or “exhaust” an unknown area with simpler objects whose area is known. The area of interest is then the sum of the areas of these simpler objects.
    🔗
    Fundamentally the Method of Exhaustion was a direct application of the limit concept we learned about in Part II. But since he did not use the modern \(\eps\)–\(\delta\) formalism the details of Eudoxus’ arguments were intricate and, well, exhausting. We will not pursue them in any depth here, but it will be illuminating to see how Archimedes’ use of how the Method of Exhaustion anticipates the modern articulation of integration, especially the Definite Integral.
    🔗
    Archimedes of Syracuse established himself as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time due in part to his innovative and dextrous use of the Method of Exhaustion to prove a diverse set of areas and volumes. Three of his results are given in Problem 20.1.1.2 and Problem 20.1.1.2 below.
    🔗
    Of his death Plutarch wrote:
    🔗
    “A Roman soldier, running upon him with a drawn sword, offered to kill him; and that Archimedes, looking back, earnestly besought him to hold his hand a little while, that he might not leave what he was then at work upon inconclusive and imperfect; but the soldier, nothing moved by his entreaty, instantly killed him.”
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 20.1.1.2 The Volume and Surface Area of a Sphere.

    Archimedes showed that both the volume and surface area of a sphere is \(2/3\) that of the circumscribed cylinder. He was so proud of this particular result that he requested that the symbol in Figure 20.1.1.3 be carved on his tombstone after his death. Legend has it that Archimedes’ tomb quickly fell into disrepair after his death and that over a century later while he was serving as Quaester in Sicily the Roman orator Cicero (107BC–44BC) located Archimedes’ tomb by recognizing this symbol on the headstone. Cicero then had the tomb repaired and restored.

    Subsection 20.1.2 The Law of the Lever and Archimedes’ Method

    The Method of Exhaustion is not a computational tool. It does not give us a means for finding say, and area or a volume. Rather It is a tool for rigorously confirming what is already known. In this it bears a strong resemblance to the modern theory of limits. While Archimedes used the Method of Exhaustion to present the results in Problem 20.1.1.2 and Problem 20.1.1.4 he actually discovered them using another procedure altogether. That procedure has been called Archimedes’ Method.
    🔗
    Archimedes describes his Method in a letter to Eratosthenes (circa 250BC):
    🔗
    “. . . I thought it might be appropriate to write down for you a special method, by means of which you will be able to recognize certain mathematical questions with the aid of mechanics. I am convinced that this is no less useful than finding the proofs of these same theorems . . . Some things, which first became clear to me by the mechanical method, were afterwards proved geometrically, because their investigation by that method does not furnish an actual demonstration. It is easier to supply the proof when we have previously acquired, by the method, some knowledge of the questions than it is to find it without any previous knowledge.”
    🔗
    🔗
    Note his reference to a “mechanical method”. In addition to his mathematics Archimedes was the first to state and prove the Law of the Lever which he then used to convince himself of the truth of many mathematical results. Having convinced himself of the truth of some proposition “mechanically” (as he would have put it) using the Law of the Lever he then proved the result rigorously using Eudoxus’ Method of Exhaustion.
    🔗

    Theorem 20.1.2.1 The Law of the Lever.

    Given two weights \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) on a lever, distances \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) from the centers of mass to the fulcrum.
    🔗
    🔗
    In his imagination Archimedes would slice two objects into infinitely thin slices and balance the corresponding slices on a balance beam (lever). If the corresponding slices balance, then the objects they came from must balance as well. In other words they were the same “size”
     1 
    “Size” is in quotes here because what we really mean is area or volume. But of course neither area nor volume can be weighed so there is an underlying assumption that area and volume are proportional to weight.
    .
    🔗

    Example 20.1.2.3.

    Consider the region \(R\) bounded by the parabola \({y=x^2} \text{,}\) and the lines \(y=0\) and \(x=a\) as in the diagram in Figure 20.1.2.4 above. We will use Archimedes’ Method and to show that the area of \(R\) is \(2/3\) of the area of \(\triangle ABC\text{.}\)
    🔗
    First we construct a vertical line at an arbitrary location, \(x\text{.}\) This creates two line segments: \(EF\) in the interior of \(\triangle ABC\text{,}\) and \(GF\) in the interior of the region \(R\) as seen in the upper diagram in Figure 20.1.2.5. Next imagine the \(x\)-axis is a balance beam with its fulcrum at the origin as seen in the lower diagram in Figure 20.1.2.5.
    🔗
    In Problem 20.1.2.7 below we ask you to show that the two segments will balance if we leave the line segment \(EF\) in place and move line segment \(GF\) to the point \((-a,0)\text{.}\) If this is so then according to Archimedes if the region \(R\) is placed \((-a,0) \) it will balance with triangle \(ABC\) left in its place as in Figure 20.1.2.6.
    🔗
    The difficulty now is that we have \(R\) located at a single point on our balance, but \(\triangle ABC\) lies above the entire line segment from \((0,0 \) to \((a,0)\) so we can’t yet apply the Law of the Lever. Fortunately it is known that the weight of \(\triangle ABC\) will act as if it is a point mass located at its center of mass. Archimedes also showed that the center of mass of will lie at the common intersection of the three lines from each vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. For \(\triangle ABC\) this is the point \(\left(\frac{2a}{3},\frac{a}{3}\right) \text{.}\) Thus according to Archimedes’ Law of the Lever we see that
    \begin{equation*} \left(\text{area of }R\right)\cdot a=\left(\text{area of } \triangle ABC\right)\cdot \frac{2}{3}a \text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} \text{volume of paraboloid }=\frac{1}{2}\text{ volume of cylinder} =\frac{\pi }{2}b^2\text{.} \end{equation*}

    DIGRESSION: Why did the Greeks Avoid the Infinite?

    Aside: Note From Bud.

    I don’t think this belongs here any more. Maybe it should go in Chapter 18 when we first talk about infinite sums?
    🔗
    Or maybe in the “Theory” part?
    🔗
    This title is somewhat simplistic, and the historical study of the infinite certainly goes beyond the scope of this book. For an historical overview of man’s pondering of the infinite, you can look at https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Infinity/. It is not that ancient Greeks or others did not ponder the infinite, but Greek mathematicians avoided using the infinite in their formal geometric proofs as the idea of an infinite object or the division of an object into infinitesimal parts lead to some paradoxical observations. One of the mathematicians/philosophers to point out such paradoxes was Zeno of Elea (490–425 BC).
    🔗
    • The Paradox of the Arrow.
      Suppose one shot an arrow into the air. If you had your cell phone back then, you might take a picture of the arrow in mid-flight. In that instant, the arrow is not moving, otherwise how could you have a still picture of it? If time is made up of instances, and at any instant, the arrow is not moving, then how can the arrow move?
      🔗
      🔗
    • Achilles and the Tortoise..
      Achilles, who is faster than a tortoise, is trying to catch said tortoise. So, Achilles runs to where the tortoise is. However, in that period of time, the tortoise has moved, so Achilles runs to where the tortoise is now. However, in that time, the tortoise has moved again. So, Achilles must run to that point, in which time the tortoise has moved again. If we continue this “forever,” will Achilles ever catch the tortoise?
      🔗
      🔗
    • The Dichotomy.
      Suppose you want to travel \(1\) yard. To do so, at some point in time you travel half the distance \(\frac{1}{2}\text{.}\) At some time after that you must travel half the remaining distance which is \(\frac{1}{2^2}=1/4\text{,}\) You must then travel half the remaining distance or \(\frac{1}{2^3}=1/8\text{.}\) Continuing this “forever” suggests that
      \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{8}+\dots =1\text{.} \end{equation*}
      Before you dismiss this as “whatever”, consider that you probably learned that \(\frac{1}{3}=.333\dots \text{.}\) This says that
      \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{3}=.333\dots = \frac{3}{10} + \frac{3}{{10}^2} + \frac{3}{{10}^3}+\dots\text{.} \end{equation*}
      You might be willing to buy this, but what if we multiply everything by \(3\text{.}\) This suggests
      \begin{equation*} 1=.999\dots =\frac{9}{10}+\frac{9}{{10}^2}+\frac{9}{{10}^3}+\dots \text{.} \end{equation*}
      Are you so willing to accept that \(.999\dots =1\text{?}\) If these are starting to give you a headache, remember that these are paradoxes which a supposed to seem contradictory. The genius of someone like Archimedes (and Newton and Leibniz) is to look past these paradoxes and use the infinite as a tool while admitted ignoring the philosophical issues. It should be noted that many issues of the infinite were settled by Georg Cantor (1845 -1918) but not without controversy in his day. There are still paradoxes, but modern mathematicians tend to be pragmatic and utilize the infinite even if we really don’t fully understand it.
      🔗
      🔗
