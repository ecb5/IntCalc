    Section 4.2 Areas in the Plane

    Because he invented what has been called the Method of Exhaustion Eudoxus of Cnidus (408BC–355BC) is considered by some to be the original inventor of Calculus — predating Newton and Leibniz by nearly two thousand years —.
    🔗
    Archimedes of Syracuse established himself as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time in part due to his innovative and dextrous use of the Method of Exhaustion to compute a diverse set of areas and volumes. His computation of the area of general Parabolic Section in particular anticipates both the definite integral and infinite series (which we will discuss in more depth in Chapter 5) so it will serve as a useful introduction to both.
    🔗

    Definition 4.2.0.2. Parabolic Section.

    A parabolic section is the region which a straight line cuts from a parabola.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.0.3.

    (a)

    We want to find the area of the parabolic section defined by the graphs of
    \begin{align*} y\amp{}=x^2 \text{ and }\\ y\amp{}=2x. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The first step is always to sketch the problem. In this case we only need to graph our two curves.
    🔗
    We want to “exhaust” the area of the section by filling it with infinitely many differential rectangles, similar to the generic rectangle in red whose width is \(\dx{x}\text{.}\) Since the upper and lower endpoints of our rectangle lie on the upper and lower bounding curves respectively, we see that the area of a typical rectangle is
    \begin{equation*} \text{Area of rectangle} = \left(2x-x^2\right)\dx{x.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice that a clearly labeled sketch ennables us to to read the area of the differential rectangle easily right off the picture. This was the point — to divide the problem into easily computable parts. This is how to set up any such problem. First understand what’s happening locally (at the infinitesimal scale).
    🔗
    Next, To compute the area of the shaded region we must add these “differential rectangles” together using the FTC. (See Problem 4.2.0.6 below.)
    \begin{equation*} \text{Total Area of the Section} = \int_{x=0}^{2}\left(2x-x^2\right)\dx{x}=\frac{4}{3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We highly recommend that you follow this pattern closely whenever you need to compute a definite integral. You want to “exhaust” the area you are interested in by filling it with figures whose area is known. Most of the time these will be rectangles. But whatever they are your first task is to generate a generic formula for their area. So focus your attention first on the local problem. Then sum the differential areas using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
    🔗
    Follow this routine on every problem, no matter how easy it may seem. This will help you to develop a habit of careful analysis. That habit will serve you well as the applications get more complicated. We (the authors) would insist but obviously we have no way to enforce the injunction. Nevertheless, we know what we are talking about. Listen to us.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    In part (a) we divided the \(x-\)axis into differentials which gave our red “differential rectangles” a vertical orientation. But of course there is nothing special about that choice. We could also have divided the \(y-\)axis into differentials which would have resulted in rectangles with a horizontal orientation as in Figure 4.2.0.5 below. Either way we should obtain the same area. What is important is that we divide the region consistently into “parallel” rectangles. (Recall Cavalieri’s “all the lines.” Our differential rectangles are essentially taking on the role of Cavalieri’s parallel lines.)
    🔗
    Figure 4.2.0.5 shows a sketch reflecting that choice. Notice that this time the (horizontally oriented) rectangles will be stacked vertically like a pile of books on a table, rather than horizontally like books on a shelf. So this time our summation will also have a vertical orientation and the summation variable is \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Notice that you cannot use Theorem 3.2.0.3 to compute a definite integral if the variable in the integrand does not match the variable in the differential.
    🔗
    Continuing, we need an expression for the area of our red rectangle. Do you see what it is? Give it some thought before reading on.
    🔗
    The left end of the rectangle is on the curve \(y=2x\) and the right end is on the curve \(y=x^2\text{.}\) The length of our rectangle will be the difference between the \(x-\)coordinates of these points. That is the area of our rectangle is
    \begin{equation*} \left(\sqrt{y}-\frac{y}{2}\right)\dx{y}. \end{equation*}
    Since the variable here is \(y\) we also need to use the \(y-\)coordinates in the indices. Thus our definite integral is:
    \begin{equation*} \text{Total Area of the Section} = \int_{y=0}^{4} \left(\sqrt{y}-\frac{y}{2}\right)\dx{y} = \frac{4}{3}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note well: In order to use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus 3.2.0.3 to compute a definite integral the variables in the integrand, the differential, and the indices must all match. This knowledge can serve as a check to make sure you have set up the definite integral correctly.
    🔗
    Since the area of the section has not changed we expect to get the same number however we compute it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 4.2.0.7. Archimedes Result By Modern Methods.

    In this problem we will use the full power of Analytic Geometry, Algebra, and a little bit of Calculus to derive the results that Archimedes had to obtain with considerably more labor and ingenuity using only Geometry.
    🔗

    (a)

    Consider the following diagram. Show that the area of \(\triangle ABC\) is
    \begin{equation*} \left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right)^3. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Hint.
    There are many ways to do this, but perhaps the simplest is to notice that \(\triangle ACD\) and \(\triangle BCD\) have the same base. What are their heights?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Set up and compute the definite integral which give the area of the parabolic sector in our sketch.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Show that your result in part (b) is exactly \(\frac{4}{3}\) of the result in part (a). This was Archimedes’ result.
    🔗
    🔗
    In the next problem we will use Archimedes results in this problem to show how his methods anticipated both the definite integral and Geometric Series.
    🔗
    🔗

    Problem 4.2.0.9. How Archimedes Did It.

    Archimedes was able to show the results of Problem 4.2.0.7 but his methods were considerably more laborious for two reasons.
    🔗
    First, he obtained the result for a completely general parabola. We only showed the result for the parabola with equation \(y=x^2\text{.}\) To generalize our result would require another major computation and would take us to far afield.
    🔗
    Second, Archimedes did not have access to algebraic notation of any kind. Thus all of the results we obtained by manipulating formulas algebraically he would have had to interpret geometrically, which is a considerably more cumberson endeavor.
    🔗
    But, having obtained the results in part (a) of Problem 4.2.0.7, he was able to obtain part (c) directly.
    🔗

    (a)

    Notice that in Figure 4.2.0.8 the lines \(\overline{AC}\) and \(\overline{BC}\) each cut off another section of the parabola.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use your result from part (a) of Problem 4.2.0.7 to show that
    \begin{equation*} \text{area of }\triangle ACF + \triangle CBE =\frac{1}{4}\text{(area of } \triangle ABC) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Compute the sum of the areas of the triangles constructed using the mid-points of \(\overline{AB}\text{,}\) \(\overline{FC}\text{,}\) \(\overline{CE}\text{,}\) and \(\overline{EB}\text{.}\) Then do it again. And then again, and continue ad infinitum, to show that the area of the parabolic section is equal to
    \begin{equation} \triangle ABC\left[1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \right].\tag{4.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    The sum in formula (4.2) is called a Geometric Series. You may have seen similar sums in the past. If so, and you know how to compute the value of this infinite sum, do that. You will find that it is equal to \(\frac{4}{3}\text{.}\) We will return to this question (how to compute the sum of a Geometric Series) in [provisional cross-reference: geometric series].
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.0.10.

    Compute the area of the following region bounded by the curves
    \begin{align*} y\amp{}=0 \text{ (the }x-\text{axis)}\\ y\amp{}=x+2\\ y\amp{}=\sqrt{4-x}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    A diagram of the essential features of this problem is given below. In the future you will be expected to provide your own. In all of mathematics there is nothing quite as useful as an aid to both understanding and calculation as a visual representation, however crude, of the problem you are working on. Always sketch your problem and label it appropriately.
    🔗
    Clearly we need to accumulate the areas of differential rectangles \(x=-2\) to \(x=4\text{,}\) so it would seem that the definite integral to be evaluated is
    \begin{equation*} \int_{x=-2}^{4}f(x)\dx{x} \end{equation*}
    where the graph of \(f(x) \) is curve in red in our diagram.
    🔗
    This is a correct understanding of the problem, but the actual computation of the integral requires a little more. In particular, notice that our function comes in two parts:
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \begin{cases} x+2 \amp{} \text{ if } -2\le{}x\lt0\\ \sqrt{4-x} \amp{} \text{ if }0\le{}x. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    That is, the nature of our function changes at \(x=0\) and we need to account for the change when we compute the integral.
    🔗

    Aside: Piecewise Functions.

    Be sure you are clear on this point: We do not have two functions, we have a single function in two parts. Which part we use depends on whether the \(x\) is greater than or less than zero.
    🔗
    In particular, the differential rectangles all have the form \((x+2)\dx{x} \) when \(x\le{}0\) and the form \(\sqrt{4-x}\dx{x}\) when \(x\ge{}0\text{.}\) Thus we have
    \begin{align*} \int_{x=-2}^{4}f(x)\dx{x}\amp{}= \int_{x=-2}^{0}f(x)\dx{x} + \int_{x=0}^{4}f(x)\dx{x}\\ \amp{}= \underbrace{\int_{x=-2}^{0}(x+2) \dx{x}}_{=2} + \underbrace{\int_{x=0}^{4}\sqrt{4-x}\dx{x}}_{=\frac{16}{3}}.\\ \amp{}=\frac{22}{3.} \end{align*}
    We need to split the integral up in this case because the differential rectangles do not have the same form across the entire interval \(\left[0, 4\right]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 4.2.0.13.

    In the previous example, we had a choices. If we chose to accumulate the (vertically oriented) differentials horizontally (sum in the \(x\) direction) the nature of the function forced us to split up the integral. If we chose to accumulate the (horizontally oriented) differential vertically (sum in the \(y\) direction) we needed to get everything in terms of \(y\) because \(y\) is the differential variable and the variables have to match before we can compute an integral. It’s nice to have a choice but we won’t always have that luxury.
    🔗
    We would like to compute the area bounded by the curves
    \begin{align*} x\amp{}=0\\ y\amp{}=\sin(x)\\ y=\cos(x) \text{ and}\\ x=\frac{5\pi}{4} \end{align*}
    as seen in Figure 4.2.0.14.
    🔗
    While in principle it is possible to use horizontally oriented differential rectangles here it should be clear that this will be much much harder than summing in the \(x\) direction. For all practical purposes there is no other choice. So, we’ll use a vertically oriented rectangles.
    🔗
    But notice that from \(x=0\) to \(x=\frac{\pi }{4}\) the height of our rectangles will be
    \begin{equation*} \cos\left(x\right)-\sin\left(x\right) \end{equation*}
    whereas from \(x=\frac{\pi }{4}\) to \(\frac{5\pi }{4}\) the height will be
    \begin{equation*} \sin\left(x\right)-\cos\left(x\right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Thus we will have to split the integral at \(x=\frac{\pi }{4}\)
    \begin{equation*} \text{Total area} = \int_{x=0}^{\frac{\pi }{4}}\cos\left(x\right)-\sin\left(x\right)\dx{x} + \int_{x=\frac{\pi}{4}}^{\frac{5\pi }{4}}\sin\left(x\right)-\cos\left(x\right)\dx{x}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
