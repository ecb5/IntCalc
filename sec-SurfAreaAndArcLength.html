    Section 4.5 Surface Area and Arc Length

    Recall that we mentioned that in his treatise On the Sphere and the Cylinder, Archimedes not only showed that the sphere had \(2/3\) the volume of its circumscribing cylinder, but it also had \(2/3\) the surface area. Again, we will not duplicate how Archimedes did it, but we will verify it with Calculus.
    🔗
    Surface area poses a bit more of a challenge than volume. This can be illustrated by the following two boxes.
    🔗
    The boxes have the same base and same height, so they have the same volume. However, the slanted box clearly has more surface and could be stretched as far as you like, so the there really is no limit to how large the surface area could be made. The slant makes a difference in surface area. To see this more carefully, we will need to first develop the notion of arc length.
    🔗

    Aside: Note to self.

    Make this a proper link.
    🔗
    You have already been exposed to arc length in the differential calculus part of this book. Recall that in looking at the catenary [Section 4.3.1], we had that the hanging chain satisfied the equation
    \begin{equation*} \dfdx{y}{x} =\frac{ws}{H} \end{equation*}
    where \(e\text{,}\) \(H\) were the weight density of the chain and horizontal tension, respectively, and \(s\) is the length of the chain from the lowest point to \((x,y)\text{.}\) We then differentiated and applied the Pythagorean Theorem to the “differential” triangle
    🔗
    We will exploit this to look at the arc length of a curve. Specifically, suppose we have a curve from point \(A=(x_1, y_1)\) to point \(B=(x_2,y_2)\) and want to compute the length of this curve \(s\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we divide this curve into infinitely small segments, then we can compute its length by integrating the lengths of these segments. In other words, we have
    \begin{equation*} s=\int_{X=A}^B \dx{s} \end{equation*}
    where \(X\) denotes a generic point \((x,y)\) on the curve. Using our infinitesimal Pythagorean Theorem, we have
    \begin{equation} s=\int_{X=A}^B\dx{s}=s=\int_{X=A}^B \sqrt{(\dx{x})^2+(\dx{y})^2}\tag{4.3} \end{equation}
    To actually compute this integral (via the Fundamental Theorem), we need to put this integral into a form that we can anti–differentiate. For example, we could put everything in terms of \(x\) or in terms of \(y\) yielding these two possibilities
    \begin{equation} s=\int_{X=A}^B \sqrt{1+\left(\dfdx{y}{x} \right)^2 }\abs{\dx{x}}\tag{4.4} \end{equation}
    \begin{equation} s =\int_{X=A}^B \sqrt{1+\left(\dfdx{x}{y} \right)^2 }\abs{\dx{y}}\tag{4.5} \end{equation}
    The absolute value is necessary since we are factoring out \(\sqrt{(\dx{x})^2}\) or \(\sqrt{(\dx{y})^2}\text{.}\)In practice, if \(x\) is always increasing then \(\dx{x}\gt 0\) and so the absolute value isn’t necessary. The same applies to \(y\text{.}\) In many applications, this will be the case, but you should be wary that it can happen.
    🔗

    Problem 4.5.0.4.

    Consider the quarter circle given by the graph of \(y=\sqrt{1-x^2}\text{,}\) where \(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \le x\le \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\text{.}\) Since the circumference of the unit circle is \(2\pi \text{,}\) the length of this quarter circle should be \(\frac{\pi }{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    (a)

    Use an integral to verify this result.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose we tried to compute the length of a quarter of the circle in the following way
    \begin{equation*} \int_{x=\left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) }^{\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) }\sqrt{\left(\dfdx{x}{y} \right)^2 +1}\dx{y}=\int_{x=\left( -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) }^{\left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) }\sqrt{1+\left(\dfdx{y}{x} \right)^2 }\dx{x} \end{equation*}
    We’d have gotten an arclength of zero. What goes wrong?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Again, there is real temptation to try to memorize formulas (4.4) and (4.5) but we urge you not to. First, why try to remember two formulas, when one just needs to remember the Pythagorean Theorem (4.3). This seems much more natural.
    🔗
    At this point, it is just a matter of determining what variable you wish to put everything in terms of. The variable doesn’t even need to be \(x\) or \(y\text{.}\) For example, suppose we have \(x=x(u)\text{,}\) and \(y=y(u)\text{,}\) with \(u_0\le u_1\text{.}\) So we don’t need to deal with absolute values, let’s assume that the parameter \(u\) is always increasing so that \(\dx{u}\gt 0\text{.}\) Then we could put everything in terms of \(u\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} s=\int_{X=A}^{B}\dx{x} = \int_{X=A}^{B}\sqrt{(\dx{x})^2+(\dx{y})^2} = \int_{X=A}^{B}\sqrt{\left(\dfdx{x}{y} \right)^2+\left(\dfdx{y}{x} \right)^2}\dx{u} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Now that we have arc length in place, let’s go back to the problem of computing the surface area of a sphere. Recalling that surface area needs to take into account the “slant” of the surface rather than just the height, let’s actually divide the surface of the sphere into infinitely small bands.
    🔗
    What we need to do is compute the surface area of this band (called a frustum of a cone). To do that, we need to find the surface area of a cone (without the bottom). With that in mind, let’s start with a cone with base radius of \(r\) and slant length \(s\text{.}\) If we slice the cone and flatten it (much like a paper cup), then we can determine the surface area of the cone, by finding the area of the sector created by flattening it.
    🔗
    By dividing this sector into infinitely many “infinitely thin” triangles, and integrating the areas of these triangles, we get that the area of the sector (and thus the cone) is given by \(\frac12 \cdot 2\pi r\cdot s=\pi rs\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Problem 4.5.0.8. The Surface Area of a Conical Frustum.

    Consider the frustum cut from the following cone.
    🔗
    Show that the surface area of the frustum is given by
    \begin{equation*} 2\pi \left(\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}\right)\cdot s=\text{ average circumference} \cdot \text{ slant length} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Armed by this, we can obtain that the surface area of a surface of revolution obtained by revolving a curve about the \(x\)-axis is given by integrating the surface areas of these infinitely thin bands.
    🔗
    As with arc length, you are free to put this integral in terms of whichever variable you wish.
    🔗

    Problem 4.5.0.14. The Surface Area of a Torus.

    In problem 4.4.0.7 we showed that the volume of a torus is equal to the area of the generating circle times the circumference of the circle formed by revolving the center of this circle about axis of the torus. One might wonder if a similar result holds for the surface area of a torus; that is, is the surface area of the torus equal to the circumference of the generating circle times the circumference of the circle formed by revolving the center of this circle about the axis of the torus? To answer this, consider the following torus generated by revolving the circle \(x^2+y^2=b^2\) about the line \(x=-a\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Find the surface area of this torus and use this to answer the above question.
    🔗
    Hint.
    You might find it easier using the parametric equations (4.6) and (4.7) instead of the equation \(x^2+y^2=b^2\) for the generating circle.
