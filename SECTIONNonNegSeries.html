    Section 2.4 Series With Nonnegative Terms

    The reason we are starting with series with nonnegative terms is that determining whether a series converges or not is typically a difficult question; determining what it converges to is typically a really difficult question. For example, we will eventually show that \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n^2}}\) converges. This was known in the early 1700’s. It took a genius like Leonard Euler to show that \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n^2}}=\frac{\pi{}^2}{6}\) in 1735.
    🔗
    In the case of something like
    \begin{gather*} .101001000100001\dots =\frac{1}{10}+\frac{1}{{10}^3}+\frac{1}{{10}^6}+\frac{1}{{10}^{10}}+\frac{1}{{10}^{15}}+\dots =\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{{10}^{1+2+\dots +n}}} \end{gather*}
    This may not even make much sense without some other context.
    🔗
    Thus, we will focus on the question of whether or not a series converges and only talk about what it converges to when it is clear what that value would be (like with a geometric series). This is where our version of the completeness axiom of the real number system will come into play.
    🔗
    Specifically, given a series \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{a_n}\) with \(a_n\ge 0\text{,}\) then by what we said in the previous section, we have
    \begin{gather*} \limit{N}{ \infty}{ \left(\sum^N_{n=0}{a_n}\right)}=a \end{gather*}
    where \(a\) is either a real number or \(a\) is infinity. In the first case, we write
    \begin{gather*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{a_n}=a \end{gather*}
    In the second case, we write
    \begin{gather*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{a_n}=\infty \end{gather*}
    🔗
    In theory, having only these two choices (bounded or unbounded) makes it sound easy, but in practice, it is much more difficult than that and we will develop some techniques for determining this. The first such test is provided by the following which actually applies to all series, not just those with nonnegative terms
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.0.1.

    If \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{a_n}\) converges, then \(\limit{n}{ \infty}{ a_n}=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To see this, suppose the series converges to the real number \(a\text{.}\) Then we have
    \begin{gather*} \limit{N}{ \infty}{ \left(\sum^N_{n=0}{a_n}\right)=a} \end{gather*}
    But we also have
    \begin{gather*} \limit{N}{ \infty}{ \left(\sum^{N-1}_{n=0}{a_n}\right)=a} \end{gather*}
    Thus
    \begin{gather*} \limit{N}{ \infty}{ a_N}=\limit{N}{ \infty}{ \left(\sum^N_{n=0}{a_n}-\sum^{N-1}_{n=0}{a_n}\right)=a-a=0} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The real power of this theorem is in its contrapositive: If \(\limit{n}{ \infty}{ a_n}\neq 0\text{,}\) then \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{a_n}\) diverges. So, for example, we know right away that \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{\frac{n}{n+1}}\) diverges since \(\limit{n}{ \infty}{ \frac{n}{n+1}\ }=1\neq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Problem 2.4.0.2.

    Use the above theorem to show that the geometric series \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{x^n}\) diverges for \(\left|x\right|\ge 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Unfortunately, things are not as simple as just determining whether the terms converge to zero or not. Consider, for example, the two series
    \begin{gather*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n}}\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n^{1.00001}}} \end{gather*}
    🔗
    On the surface, there doesn’t appear to be much difference between these two series. After all, if we just sum the first 1000 terms in each, we get the following approximations.
    \begin{gather*} \sum^{1000}_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n}}\approx 7.48547\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n^{1.00001}}}\approx 7.48523 \end{gather*}
    🔗
    Based upon this it would seem that these two series should converge to values which are close to each other. However, the first series, called the harmonic series, is infinite and the second series, known as a \(p\) series, converges to a finite number (approximately 100,000.5772). In fact, the harmonic series diverges so slowly that one would need to add approximately \(1.509\times {10}^{43}\) terms to get it to surpass 100. This is beyond the computing power of even the fastest computers. In fact, since it diverges to infinity, it will eventually pass that number as well. What is the difference to make these behave so differently? It turns out that a test involving integrals works well here.
    🔗
    Before we get into the statement of the integral test, we should mention something about subscripts, or more specifically the starting value for our subscripts. Since we are only concerned with convergence and not necessarily the value the series converges to, then where we start our summation doesn’t affect convergence (only the value of the series). For example, if we have a convergent series
    \begin{gather*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=0}{a_n}=a \end{gather*}
    Then
    \begin{gather*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{a_n}=a-a_0\sum^{\infty }_{n=100}{a_n}=a-(a_1+\dots +a_{99}) \end{gather*}
    so the starting subscript does not affect convergence, only the actual value. Since often the question about a particular series comes down to convergence and not the value it converges to, mathematicians sometimes become lazy and write a series as \(\sum{a_n}\) with the understanding that the limits of summation will be included if questions go beyond just convergence. With this in mind, let’s state the integral test.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.4.0.3. The Integral Test.

    Suppose \(f(x)\) is nonnegative decreasing (actually nonincreasing) integrable function defined on \([1,\infty )\text{.}\) If
    \begin{equation*} \int^{\infty }_{x=1}{f(x)}dx\lt{}\infty \end{equation*}
    then
    \begin{equation*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{f(n)}\lt{}\infty \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If
    \begin{equation*} \int^{\infty }_{x=1}{f\left(x\right)dx}=\infty \end{equation*}
    then
    \begin{equation*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{f\left(n\right)}=\infty \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Before we prove that this is true, let’s apply it to the harmonic series \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{\frac{1}{n}}\) we mentioned earlier. If we consider the function \(f\left(x\right)=\frac{1}{x}\) on \([1,\infty )\text{,}\) then it satisfies all the hypotheses of the integral test, namely, it is nonnegative, decreasing, and integrable. Furthermore
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \int^{\infty }_{x=1}{\frac{1}{x}}dx=\limit{B}{ \infty}{ \left(\int^B_{x=1}{\frac{1}{x}dx}\right)}=\limit{B}{ \infty}{ \left({\ln \left(B\right)}\right)}=\infty \end{gather*}
    Thus, the harmonic series \(\sum{\frac{1}{n}}=\infty \) .
    🔗

    Problem 2.4.0.4.

    Suppose \(p\) is a fixed positive number. Use the integral test to show that if \(p\lt{}1\text{,}\) then \(\sum{\frac{1}{n^p}}\) diverges and if \(p>1\text{,}\) then \(\sum{\frac{1}{n^p}}\) converges.
    🔗
    🔗
    The series \(\sum{\frac{1}{n^p}}\)is called a \(p\) series and given what we have above it converges when \(p>1\) and diverges when \(p\le 1\text{,}\) though typically, we do not know what it converges to when it does.
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} \int^{\infty }_{x=1}{g(x)}dx=\infty \end{equation*}
    but
    \begin{equation*} \sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{g\left(n\right)} \end{equation*}
    is finite.
    🔗
    🔗
    In the integral test, we compared our series to an improper integral, but what about comparing two series? It turns out that this relatively straightforward test is a linchpin for a number of other tests.
    🔗
    \textbf{Theorem. The Comparison Test.} Suppose that \(0\le a_n\le b_n\) for all \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{b_n}\)converges, then \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{a_n}\)converges.
    🔗
    If \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{a_n}\)diverges then \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{b_n}\)diverges.
    🔗
    To see the first part of this, notice that the sequence of partial sums \(\left(\sum^N_{n=1}{a_n}\right)\) is an increasing sequence which is bounded above by whatever the series \(\sum{b_n}\) converges to. Thus, it must converge by our completeness axiom of the real number system. The second part of the Comparison Test is the contrapositive of the first part, so it is automatically true.
    🔗
    To see this in action, consider the series \(\sum{\frac{1}{n^2+3n+6}}\text{,}\) since \(0\le \frac{1}{n^2+3n+6}\le \frac{1}{n^2}\) and the series \(\sum{\frac{1}{n^2}}\) converges, then \(\sum{\frac{1}{n^2+3n+6}}\) must converge by the comparison test. The hard part of utilizing the comparison test is trying to find a series to compare it to. For example, we also know \(0\le \frac{1}{n^2+3n+6}\le \frac{1}{3n}\text{.}\) However, the fact that the larger series \(\sum{\frac{1}{3n}}\) diverges tells us nothing about the smaller series. Typically, it takes experience and exposure to a wide variety of series to decide what is a candidate for making a comparison.
    🔗
    One thing that might help is to notice that a finite number of terms does not affect the convergence or divergence of a series, then we only need our inequalities to occur for sufficiently large \(n\text{.}\) More precisely we have a stronger statement of the comparison test.
    🔗
    \textbf{Theorem. The Comparison Test.} Suppose there is a real number \(N\) such that that \(0\le a_n\le b_n\) for all \(n\ge N\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{b_n}\)converges, then \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{a_n}\)converges.
    🔗
    If \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{a_n}\)diverges then \(\sum^{\infty }_{n=1}{b_n}\)diverges.
    🔗
    To see this in action, consider the series \(\sum{\frac{n}{n^2+3n+6}}\text{.}\) Notice that if \(n=5\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} n^2=25>21=3\cdot 5+6=3n+6 \end{equation*}
    Since the slope of the curve \(y=x^2\) exceeds the slope of the curve \(y=3x+6\) for \(x\ge 5\text{,}\) then for \(n\ge 5\text{,}\) \(n^2>3n+6\text{.}\) Thus
    \begin{equation*} 0\lt{}n^2+3n+6\lt{}n^2+n^2=2n^2 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 0\lt{}\frac{1}{2n^2}\lt{}\frac{1}{n^2+3n+6} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 0\lt{}\frac{1}{2n}=\frac{n}{2n^2}\lt{}\frac{n}{n^2+3n+6} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(\sum{\frac{1}{2n}}\) diverges, then by the comparison test, \(\sum{\frac{n}{n^2+3n+6}}\) diverges.
    🔗
    Since this seemed to come out of nowhere, let’s try and get into the author’s head as to what went on. By experience, the author knew that eventually, \(n^2\) would dominate the \(3n+6\text{,}\) so this lead the author to try to ignore \(3n+6\text{.}\) This would lead to something that looked like the harmonic series (or at least a constant multiple times it) which diverges. So, the author expected the series to diverge. To make things more precise, the author knew that eventually we would have \(n^2>3n+6\text{.}\) To see where this happened, he set \(x^2-3x-6=0\) to obtain \(x=\frac{3\pm \sqrt{33}}{2}\text{.}\) Given that he knew the nature of the parabola \(y=x^2+3x+6\text{,}\) he knew that he only needed to choose a number larger than \(\frac{3+\sqrt{33}}{2}\) to get \(n^2>3n+6\text{.}\) The number \(\frac{3+\sqrt{49}}{2}=5\) was a convenient choice. Notice again that the hard part was not the comparison test per se but finding an appropriate comparison series. This took experience and a feel for what series converged and diverged. Here are some exercises for you to practice to get such experience.
    🔗
    \textbf{Insert drill on comparison test.}
    🔗
    \textbf{}
    🔗
    To make this notion of ignoring the terms \(3n+6\) in the pervious example a bit more precise, notice that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \limit{n}{ \infty}{ \frac{\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)}{\left(\frac{n}{n^2+3n+6}\right)}=\limit{n}{ \infty}{ \frac{n^2+3n+6}{n^2}}=1} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This says that for \(n\) large, \(\frac{n}{n^2+3n+6}\approx \frac{1}{n}\text{.}\) This is what led the author to consider the harmonic series. This idea leads to a variation of the comparison test which involves limits. The proof of this still uses the comparison test.
    🔗
    \textbf{Theorem. The Limit Comparison Test. }Suppose \(a_n,b_n>0\) and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \limit{n}{ \infty}{ \frac{a_n}{b_n}}=c\mathrm{where}0\lt{}c\lt{}\infty \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Then \(\sum{a_n}\) and \(\sum{b_n}\) either both converge or both diverge. That is if one of them converges, the other does and if one of them diverges, the other one does.
    🔗
    To see that this follows from the comparison test we will show that if \(\sum{b_n}\) converges, then \(\sum{a_n}\) converges. To see this, notice that since \(\limit{n}{ \infty}{ \frac{a_n}{b_n}}=c>0\)then for \(n\) large enough \(\frac{a_n}{b_n}\lt{}2c\text{,}\) so that \(0\lt{}a_n\lt{}2cb_n\text{.}\) Since \(\sum{2cb_n}=2c\sum{b_n}\) converges, then by the comparison test \(\sum{a_n}\)converges. To show that \(\sum{a_n}\) converging implies \(\sum{b_n}\)converges, notice that \(0\lt{}\frac{1}{c}\lt{}\infty \text{.}\)
    🔗
    \textbf{Problem.} Use the above observation and the fact that \(\limit{n}{ \infty}{ \frac{b_n}{a_n}}=\frac{1}{c}\)to show that if \(\sum{a_n}\) converges, then \(\sum{b_n}\) converges.
    🔗
    Since we showed that \(\sum{a_n}\) converges if and only if \(\sum{b_n}\) converges, then it follows that if one of these diverges then the other does as well.
    🔗
    Notice that the limit comparison test streamlines the process by eliminating the algebraic inequalities that we needed in our example of the comparison test. However, given a series to test for convergence, it still takes experience to determine a series to compare it to.
