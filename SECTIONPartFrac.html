    Section 2.2 The Partial Fraction Decomposition

    Mathematics is the art of avoiding computation.
    🔗
    ―Brockway McMillan
    🔗

    Motivating Example 2.2.0.1.

    In part (c) of Problem 1.2.0.8 you were faced with the problem of computing
    \begin{equation} \int\frac{1}{u(u+1)} \dx{u}\tag{2.31} \end{equation}
    which is relatively simple to do if you know that
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{u(u+1)}=\frac1u-\frac{1}{u+1} \text{.}\tag{2.32} \end{equation}
    This is why we had you confirm formula (2.32) in part (d) of Problem 1.2.0.8. But without this decomposition the integral would have been daunting at best. The process by which we compute the decomposition \(\frac{1}{u(u+1)}=\frac1u-\frac{1}{u+1}\) is known as the Partial Fraction Decomposition (PFD). The PFD is useful in a variety of situations beyond the computation of integrals, but it is a purely algebraic operation so we will not be examining its deeper implications. For us, right now, it is only useful as a way to decompose integrals like formula (2.31).
    🔗

    (a)

    We will build up the computational scheme slowly, starting with simple decompositions. We’ll begin by making the simplest possible guess for the decomposition so we assume that the decomposition has the form:
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{u(u+1)} = \frac{A}{u} + \frac{B}{u+1} \text{.}\tag{2.33} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Does this seem to you like a reasonable guess? Explain.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Multiply both sides of equation (2.33) by \(u(u+1)\) to clear the fractions. Then show that \(A\) and \(B\) must satisfy the equations:
    \begin{align*} A+B\amp{}=0\\ A\amp{}=1 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Solve the equations in part (b) and confirm (again) that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{u(u+1)}-\frac{1}{u}-\frac{1}{u+1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.1 Simple Decompositions: Heaviside’s Cover–up Method (HCUM)

    Suppose we have an expression of the form \(\frac{1}{(x-a)(x-b)}\) where \(a\text{,}\) and \(b\) are known constants, and \(a\neq b\text{.}\) This is a slight generalization of Motivating Example 2.2.0.1. To see that let \(a=0\) and \(b=-1\text{.}\) The PFD says that there are constants \(A\) and \(B\) such that as
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{(x-a)(x-b)}=\frac{A}{x-a}+\frac{B}{x-b}\text{.}\tag{2.34} \end{equation}
    Our task is to find values for \(A\) and \(B\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We note of the following facts which will be useful in Subsection 2.2.3.
    • Equation (2.34) is an identity: Once the correct values of \(A\) and \(B\) are found it is true for every value of \(x\) (except \(x=a\) and \(x=b\) of course).
      🔗
    • The right side of equation (2.34) consists of rational functions where the degree of the numerator is one less than the degree of the denominator. This is a rather bombastic way to state that fact since the numerators are all constants (polynomials of degree zero) and the denominators are all linear (polynomials of degree one). But the importance of this observation will become clear in Subsection 2.2.3.
      🔗
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.1.3.

    The formulas for \(A\) and \(B\) derived in Problem 2.2.1.2 represent a complete solution of the decomposition of a rational function with \(1\) in the numerator and two linear factors in the denominator. It was straightforward, if a bit tedious. But suppose the denominator has three distinct factors:
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{\left(x-a\right)\left(x-b\right)\left(x-c\right)}=\frac{A}{x-a}+\frac{B}{x-b}+\frac{C}{x-c}\text{.}\tag{2.35} \end{equation}
    Clear the fractions, gather the coefficients of \(x^2\text{,}\) \(x\text{,}\) and the constant term to show that \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\) must satisfy the equations:
    \begin{align*} A+B+C\amp{}=1\\ (b+c)A+(a+c)B+(a+b)C\amp{}=0\\ (bc)A+(ac)B+(ab)C\amp{}=0 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The way we have computed the PFD so far has the advantage that it is very simple to understand. We simply clear the fractions and compare the coefficients to generate a set of linear equations to solve. But solving those the equations is tedious even in the simplest cases. And the more factors there are in the denominator the worse it gets. We would like to find a more efficient method, even if it is a little harder to understand why it works.
    🔗
    To simplify things a bit look again at equation (2.34). As before we clear the fractions by multiplying both sides by \((x-a)(x-b)\text{.}\) This results in
    \begin{equation} 1=A\left(x-b\right)+B\left(x-a\right)\text{.}\tag{2.36} \end{equation}
    Equation (2.36), like equation (2.34), is an identity which means that it is true regardless of the value of \(x\text{.}\) So we need only select a convenient value of \(x\text{.}\) Letting \(x=a\) we get
    \begin{equation*} 1=A\left(a-b\right)+\underbrace{B\left(a-a\right)}_{=0} \end{equation*}
    so that \(A=\frac{1}{a-b}\text{.}\) Similarly \(B=\frac{1}{b-a}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.1.4.

    Redo each of the problems in Problem 2.2.1.1 by choosing a convenient value for \(x\) in equation (2.36). Confirm that you get the same answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    When he was computing some PFDs related to his work with differential equations Oliver Heaviside (1850—1925) made the following observation: Look closely at the numerators of the fractions the right side of
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{(x-a)(x-b)}= \frac{\frac{1}{a-b}}{x-a}+\frac{\frac{1}{b-a}}{x-b} \text{.}\tag{2.37} \end{equation}
    Do you see that the numerator above \(x-a\) is what we would get if on the left side we “cover up” the factor \(x-a\) and replace \(x\) with \(a\text{?}\)
    🔗
    That is, if \(\frac{1}{(x-a)(x-b)}= \frac{A}{x-a}+\frac{B}{x-b}\) then
    \begin{equation*} A=\eval{\frac{1}{(\text{covered})(x-b)}}{x}{a}=\frac{1}{a-b}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Similarly
    \begin{equation*} B=\eval{\frac{1}{(x-a)(\text{covered})}}{x}{b}=\frac{1}{b-a}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This trick is known as Heaviside’s Cover–up Method (HCUM) and it will work for any number of linear factors in the denomiator as long as they are distinct. Heaviside’s Method is the simplest way we know to compute the coefficients of the PFD of the rational functions we’ve seen so far.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 The Logistic Equation, Redux

    When we analyzed the [cross-reference to target(s) "EQUATIONLogEq" missing or not unique] in [cross-reference to target(s) "SECTIONpopulation-dynamics" missing or not unique] we were unable to find a formula for \(P(t)\text{.}\) But, by analyzing the IVP directly we were able to generate a “qualitative” graph of \(P(t)\text{.}\) That is, we were able to get a general sense of the shape of the graph of \(P(t)\) even if the coordinates of particular points were unavailable.
    🔗
    While a qualitative graph is better than nothing, it would be more useful to have an explicit formula for \(P(t)\text{,}\) if we can find it. With integration we now have the tool we need to find such a formula.
    🔗
    Starting with the Logistic Equation,
    \begin{align} \dfdx{P}{t} \amp{}= 0.1P \left( 1-\frac{P}{100} \right),\amp{}P(0)\amp{}=10,\tag{2.40} \end{align}
    the first step is to separate the variables:
    \begin{equation} \frac{\dx{P}}{P(100-P)}=0.001\dx{t}\text{.}\tag{2.41} \end{equation}
    If we can integrate both sides of equation (2.41) and then solve for \(P\) we will have our formula for \(P(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Integrating the right sides presents no difficulties:
    \begin{equation} \int 0.001\dx{t} = 0.001t+C\text{.}\tag{2.42} \end{equation}
    🔗
    But at our current level of skill with integration the only option that presents itself for integrating the left side is to to complete the square in the denominator and apply the appropriate trigonometric substitution.
    🔗

    Problem 2.2.2.1.

    By completing the square in the denominator and making the substitution \(P-50=50\sin (\theta )\) integrate the left side of equation (2.41) and show that
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{50}\ln \left[ \sqrt{\frac{100-P}{P}} \right] = 0.001t+C\text{.}\tag{2.43} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    As you have just seen, integrating the left side of equation (2.41) is entirely within the range of our current skill set but it is rather daunting. And we’re not even finished yet. We must still solve equation (2.43) for \(P(t)\text{.}\) Before we do that we’d really like to find a simpler way to compute the integral.
    🔗
    To be consistent with what follows we observe that we can rewrite equation (2.41) as
    \begin{equation} \frac{\dx{P}}{P\left(P-100\right)}=-0.001 \dx{t}\tag{2.44} \end{equation}
    since \(\frac{\dx{P}}{P\left(P-100\right)} = -\frac{\dx{P}}{P\left(100-P\right)}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Integrating the right–hand side poses no problem as we would get
    \begin{equation} \int{\frac{\dx{P}}{P\left(P-100\right)}}=-0.001\ t+C\tag{2.45} \end{equation}
    To compute the left–hand side, notice that
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{P\left(P-100\right)}=\frac{-\frac{1}{100}}{P}+\frac{\frac{1}{100}}{P-100}\tag{2.46} \end{equation}
    🔗

    🔗
    This is certainly easier to deal with than a trigonometric substitution, but it begs the question, “Where did equation (2.46) \(\frac{1}{P\left(P-100\right)}=-\frac{\frac{1}{100}}{P}+\frac{\frac{1}{100}}{P-100}\) come from?” It is one thing to verify such an identity, but it is quite another to actually formulate one.
    🔗
    We found equation (2.46) by an algebraic technique called the Partial Fraction Decomposition or PFD. There are several places in mathematics where it is useful to break a single rational expresstion into the sum of simpler rational expressions. We will be using it to simplify the integration when the integrand is a rational function.

    Problem 2.2.2.3.

It is important to know what are the simplifications are so we know the conditions under which the model is unrealistic. For example, [cross-reference to target(s) "EQUATIONFishery1" missing or not unique] works fine in the short term but in the long term it predicts explosive growth which is not sustainable.     🔗

    \begin{equation} \dfdx{P}{t}=0.1P\left(1-\frac{P}{100}\right)\left(1-\frac{10}{P}\right),\tag{2.47} \end{equation}

    Subsection 2.2.3 Advanced Decompositions

    Subsubsection Linear Factors

    Example 2.2.3.1.
    Heaviside’s method, as we have used it so far, will not work on the rational function \(\frac{x^2}{(x-2)(x-3)}\text{.}\) If you try to apply it you’ll get
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{(x-2)(x-3)} = \frac{-4}{x-2}+\frac{9}{x-3} \end{equation*}
    which is wrong. Try it and see.
    🔗
    The difficulty here is that, as a practical matter, the PFD requires that the degree of the numerator of our rational function to be strictly less than the degree of the denominator. (This is somewhat analogous to putting a fraction in “lowest terms.”) In this example the degree of the numerator is
    \begin{equation*} \deg\left((x-2)(x-3)\right)=\deg\left(x^2-5x+6\right)=2 \end{equation*}
    which is the same as the degree of the denominator: \(\deg\left(x^2\right)=2\text{.}\) So the PFD, as we’ve used it so far, doesn’t apply.
    🔗
    At first this looks like it will be a major obstacle but it really isn’t too bad. There are two ways we can proceed from here.
    🔗
    Dividing First: The first is simply to do the polynomial division indicated. In practice that means multiplying out the denominator first and then dividing:
    \begin{align} \frac{x^2}{(x-2)(x-3)} = \frac{x^2}{x^2-5x+6} \amp{}= 1+ \frac{5x-6}{x^2-5x+6}\notag\\ \amp{}=1+\frac{5x-6}{(x-2)(x-3)}\tag{2.48} \end{align}
    🔗
    From there we can compute the PFD of \(\frac{5x-6}{(x-2)(x-3)}\) as before, finally obtaining
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{x^2-5x+6} = 1+\frac{-4}{x-2}+\frac{9}{x-3}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Dividing Last: The second is to factor out the numerator:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{(x-2)(x-3)} = x^2 \left[ \frac{1}{(x-2)(x-3)} \right] \text{,} \end{equation*}
    compute the PFD of \(\frac{1}{(x-2)(x-3)}\) as before. This gives
    \begin{align} \frac{x^2}{(x-2)(x-3)} \amp{}= x^2 \left[ \frac{-1}{x-2}+\frac{1}{x-3} \right]\notag\\ \amp{}=\frac{-x^2}{x-2}+\frac{x^2}{x-3}\tag{2.49}\\ \amp{}= -x-2-\frac{4}{x-2} +x+3+\frac{9}{x-3}\notag\\ \amp{}=1-\frac{4}{x-2}+\frac{9}{x-3}.\notag \end{align}
    🔗
    If you are uncomfortable with long division of polynomials, we can accomplish the same thing using a substitution. At the point where we have
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2}{\left(x-2\right)\left(x-3\right)}=-\frac{x^2}{x-2}+\frac{x^2}{x-3} \end{equation*}
    if we let \(y=x-2\) in the first fraction and \(z=x-3\) in the second fraction, then we have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{x^2}{\left(x-2\right)\left(x-3\right)}\amp{}=-\frac{{\left(y+2\right)}^2}{y}+\frac{{\left(z+3\right)}^2}{z}\\ \amp{}=-\frac{y^2+4y+4}{y}+\frac{z^2+6z+9}{z}\\ \amp{}=-y-4-\frac{4}{y}+z+6+\frac{9}{z}\\ \amp{}=-x+2-4-\frac{4}{x-2}+x-3+6+\frac{9}{x-3}\\ \amp{}=1-\frac{4}{x-2}+\frac{9}{x-3} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Whether you divide first or divide last is entirely your choice, so choose whichever you are most comfortable with and use it. But be flexible. Both methods work. Use the one that feels more natural to you.

    There are two parts to computing the PFD of a rational function. First we must determine the form of the decomposition and then we must compute the unknown coefficients. From our discussion so far we know the form if the denominator is a product of distinct linear factors, and we have a number of ways to compute the coefficients involved. Being mathematicians, and therefore quite lazy, the authors’ inclination is to use the more clever, less tedious approach whenever possible.

    🔗

    🔗

    🔗

    🔗
    As you have seen there are many ways to attack the PFD of a rational function. The good news is that you can use any algebraic technique to determine the coefficients. Unfortunately that is also the bad news.
    🔗
    In this section we (the authors) are building up a procedure for the PFD which is simple, both to understand and to implement, but it is not the only known procedure. And the PFD is not Calculus, it is Algebra. So for us it is simply a way to manipulate an integral into a form we can compute. So if you know, or can find, a better method for computing the PFD go ahead and use it.
    🔗
    Having so many options can make your head spin, especially if you are using techniques without fully understanding why they work. So think before you act and proceed deliberately. With experience you will begin to see patterns and there will be fewer false starts.
    🔗
    The theorem underlying all of this is Theorem 2.2.3.7 which we now state formally.
    🔗
    Theorem 2.2.3.7. The Partial Fraction Decomposition (PFD).
    If \(P(x)\text{,}\) \(Q(x)\text{,}\) and \(R(x)\) are polynomials having no non–trivial common factors with \(\deg(P)\lt\deg(Q)+ \deg(R)\) then there exist polynomials \(A(x)\) and \(B(x)\text{,}\) with \(\deg(A)\lt\deg(Q)\) and \(\deg(B)\lt\deg(R)\text{,}\) such that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)\cdot{}R(x)} = \frac{A(x)}{Q(x)}+\frac{B(x)}{R(x)}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, suppose we had the rational function
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2-x+2}{x{\left(x+1\right)}^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    By theorem 19.2.2.11, we know we can write this as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2-x+2}{x{\left(x+1\right)}^2}=\frac{A}{x}+\frac{Bx+C}{{\left(x+1\right)}^2} \end{equation*}
    but it is more convenient to write it as
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x^2-x+2}{x{\left(x+1\right)}^2}=\frac{A}{x}+\frac{B(x+1)+C}{{\left(x+1\right)}^2}=\frac{A}{x}+\frac{B}{x+1}+\frac{C}{{\left(x+1\right)}^2} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    🔗
    Example 2.2.3.10. The PFD With Repeated Linear Factors.
    Let’s finish up the decomposition of \(\frac{x}{(x-1)^2(x+2)^2}\) from part (b) of Problem 2.2.3.9. From the HCUM You should have found that \(B=\frac19\) and \(D=\frac{-2}{9}\) so that
    \begin{equation*} \displaystyle \frac{x}{(x-1)^2(x+2)^2}= \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{\frac19}{(x-1)^2} +\frac{C}{x+2} -\frac{\frac29}{(x+2)^2} \end{equation*}
    When we reached this point in Example 2.2.3.3 (see part (b) of Problem 2.2.3.4) our next step was to simply choose a value for \(x\text{.}\) We could do that here as well but because we now have two unknowns we’d have to choose two distinct values for \(x\) and generate a system of two coupled equations to solve. We’ve already seen that there are more efficient methods for computing the PFD than solving a system of equations.
    🔗
    Instead we’ll isolate all of the terms with our unknowns on one side of the equation, thus:
    \begin{equation} \displaystyle \frac{x}{(x-1)^2(x+2)^2} - \frac{\frac19}{(x-1)^2} +\frac{\frac29}{(x+2)^2} = \frac{A}{x-1} +\frac{C}{x+2}\tag{2.51} \end{equation}
    🔗
    It is clear that the right side of equation (2.51) is the PFD of some rational function whose denominator is \((x-1)(x+2)\text{.}\) That is, if we were asked to begin the process of computing the PFD of
    \begin{equation*} \frac{P(x)}{(x-1)(x+2)} \end{equation*}
    for some polynomial \(P(x)\) then our first step would be to write down the right side of equation (2.51). Therefore it must be that
    \begin{equation} \displaystyle \frac{x}{(x-1)^2(x+2)^2} - \frac{\frac19}{(x-1)^2} +\frac{\frac29}{(x+2)^2} = \frac{P(x)}{(x-1)(x+2)}\tag{2.52} \end{equation}
    for some polynomial \(P(x)\text{,}\) and if we can find that polynomial we can compute \(A\) and \(C\) by the HCUM.
    🔗
    To find \(P(x)\text{,}\) we first clear the fraction on the right leaving,
    \begin{equation} \frac{x}{(x-1)(x+2)} - \frac19 \left( \frac{x+2}{x-1} \right) +\frac29 \left( \frac{x-1}{x+2} \right) = P(x)\tag{2.53} \end{equation}
    It is tempting to find a common denominator and add the three fractions on the left together. Since we have to end up with the polynomial \(P(x)\) it must be that once the addition is done there will be a factor in the top that will cancel with the common denominator.
    🔗
    That will work. Give it a try if you want. Good luck.
    🔗
    But we know that \(P(x)\) is a polynomial and we can make very effective use of that knowledge as follows.
    🔗
    If we perform this division in the second term on the left we get
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x+2}{x-1} = 1 +\frac{3}{x-1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Similarly if we perform the division in the third term we get \(\frac{x-1}{x+2}= 1-\frac{3}{x+2}\text{.}\) Putting these back into equation (2.53) we have
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{(x-1)(x+2)} - \frac19 \left( 1 +\frac{3}{x-1} \right) +\frac29 \left( 1-\frac{3}{x+2} \right) = P(x) \end{equation*}
    or
    \begin{equation} \underbrace{\left( \frac{x}{(x-1)(x+2)} -\frac{\frac13}{x-1} -\frac{\frac23}{x+2}\right)}_{=0} + \frac19 = P(x)\text{.}\tag{2.54} \end{equation}
    Since \(P(x)\) must be a polynomial it follows that the expression in the parentheses — everything that is not a polynomial — must be equal to zero, as we’ve indicated above.
    🔗
    Thus \(P(x)=\frac19{}\text{.}\) To finish the decomposition we place this in to equation (2.52) and observe that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\frac19 }{(x-1)(x+2)} = \frac{A}{x-1}+\frac{C}{x+2} \end{equation*}
    and from the HCUM we see that \(A=\frac{1}{27} \) and \(C=-\frac{1}{27}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Having found all of the constants we conclude from equation (2.51) that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x}{(x-1)^2(x+2)^2}= \frac{\frac{1}{27}}{x-1} + \frac{\frac19 }{(x-1)^2} -\frac{\frac{1}{27}}{x+2} -\frac{\frac29 }{(x+2)^2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Naturally, it isn’t always that simple. If the degree of any (or all) of the numerators in equation (2.54) was not less than it’s denominator then we’d have had to compute the division in order to recover the polynomial part of each. But then the sum of the remainders would necessarily be zero so they can be ignored just as we ignored \(\frac{x}{(x-1)(x+2)} -\frac{\frac13}{x-1} -\frac{\frac23}{x+2}\) above.

    Subsubsection Irreducible Quadratic Factors

    So far whenever there was a quadratic factor in the denominator we were able to either factor it into two distinct linear factors (e.g. \((x^2-x+6) = (x+2)(x-3)\) or into one such factor, squared, e.g. \((x^2+6x+9)=(x+3)^2\text{.}\) Unfortunately we cannot yet compute the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int \frac{1}{(x-2)(x^2+1)}\dx{x}\text{,} \end{equation*}
    because the factor \(x^2+1\) cannot be factored (over the real numbers). So we’ll need to extend our procedure to account for this situation.
    🔗
    Aside: Comment.
    But it can be factored over the complex numbers. We will address this at the end of this section.
    🔗
    From our previous work we know that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{(x-2)(x^2+1)}= \frac{A}{x-2}+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+1} \end{equation*}
    so by the HCUM \(A=\frac15 \text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{(x-2)(x^2+1)}= \frac{\frac15}{x-2}+ \frac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As we keep repeating this is an identity; it is true regardless of the value of \(x\) . If we isolate the expression \(Bx+C\) on the right side of the equation then the left side will reduce to a linear expression in \(x\text{.}\) We can then simply compare the coefficients.
    🔗
    Multiplying both sides by \(x^2+1\) we have
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{x-2}=\frac15 \left(\frac{x^2+1}{x-2}\right)+(Bx+C) \end{equation*}
    and isolating \(Bx+C\) gives
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{x-2}-\frac15\left( \frac{x^2+1}{x-2}\right)=Bx+C\text{,} \end{equation*}
    and simplifying the expression on the left we see that
    \begin{equation*} Bx+C=-\frac15x -\frac25 \text{,} \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation} \frac{1}{(x-2)(x^2+1)}= \frac{\frac15}{x-2}- \frac{\frac15x}{x^2+1} -\frac{\frac25}{x^2+1}\text{.}\tag{2.55} \end{equation}

    Reasoning by analogy it seems reasonable to speculate that the PFD of \(\frac{1}{x^2(x^2+1)^3(x^2+9)(x-5)} \) will be
    \begin{align} \frac{1}{x^2(x^2+1)^3(x^2+9)(x-5))} = \frac{A}{x}+\frac{B}{x^2}+\amp{}\frac{Cx+D}{x^2+1}+\frac{Ex+F}{(x^2+1)^2}\tag{2.56}\\ +\frac{Gx+H}{(x^2+1)^3} \amp{}+ \frac{Ix+J}{x^2+9}+\frac{K}{x-5},\notag \end{align}
    and that is correct. Equation (2.56) is an example of the most general form that a Partial Fraction Decomposition can have. Beyond that they just get bigger. The good news is that we are done.
    🔗
    The really good news is that we won’t be asking you to compute this decomposition. On the other hand, if you would like to challenge your understanding try computing this decomposition using the methods we’ve discussed. Do it some time when you have an afternoon and evening free. It will take a while.
