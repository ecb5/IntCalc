    Section 3.1 Parametric Functions and Integration

    We first encountered parametric functions in [cross-reference to target(s) "SECTIONThinkingDynamically" missing or not unique]. Because we had more fundamental matters to investigate at the time. We didn’t examine parametric functions at the time.
    🔗
    A little later in [cross-reference to target(s) "SECTIONSelfIntersectCurve" missing or not unique] we investigated some simple parametric functions in a little more depth but not much. It would be easy to have the impression that parametric functions are an unimportant, or secondary tool. That is very untrue. In many ways it is far more commonplace to describe most natural phenomena parametrically. To see why consider [cross-reference to target(s) "FIGUREChopperFlightPath" missing or not unique], reproduced below:
    🔗
    One natural way to describe the position of the ball at time, \(t\text{,}\) is to use the horizontal position: \(x(t)\text{,}\) and the vertical position: \(y(t)\text{,}\) as Cartesian coordinates as usual:
    \begin{equation*} P(t)= \ParamEqTwo{x(t)}{y(t)}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Aside: Parametrization by Arc–length.

    The length of the path at time \(t\text{:}\) \(s(t)\) is called parametrization by arc–length. Since \(s(t)\) only gives the distance traveled along the path parametrization by arc–length is not useful for describing the shape of the balls’ flight path. It can be useful in other contexts though.
    🔗
    As we saw in [cross-reference to target(s) "SECTIONSelfIntersectCurve" missing or not unique] a parameterization for our falling ball is
    \begin{equation*} P(t)= \ParamEqTwo{x(t)}{y(t)}=\ParamEqTwo{t}{-4.9t^2} \end{equation*}
    so the slope of the curve is
    \begin{equation*} \dfdx{y}{x} =\frac{\dx{t}}{-9.8t\dx{t}} =-\frac{1}{9.8t}\dfdx{t}{t} =-\frac{1}{9.8t}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Aside: Cancelling Differentials.

    As we’ve seen the cancellation \(\dfdx{t}{t}=1\) is not strictly rigorous. In particular higher order differentials it can be very problematic as we saw in the Digression [cross-reference to target(s) "DIGRESSIONHereBeDragons" missing or not unique]. But it is a powerful “way of thinking” that is still used by engineers, physicists, and other scientists so it would be silly not to use it. Be aware however that it lacks rigor. Always check your computations.
    🔗
    Newton’s fluxions and the accompanying notation avoids this problem entirely since
    \begin{equation*} \dfdx{y}{x}=\frac{\dot{y}}{\dot{x}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice that when we parametrize with respect to a third variable (\(t\) in this case) \(\dfdx{y}{x}\) is given as a function of that third variable, not as a function of \(x\) as we’ve grown accustomed to. This can make things a little harder to think about but the third variable also gives us more flexibilty. This can be very helpful in the context of integration.
    🔗
    Recall that fluxion \(\dfdx{y}{t}=\dot{y}\) must be thought of as a rate of change. For instance when \(t\) represents time \(\dfdx{y}{t}\) \(\left(\dot{y}\right)\) is a vertical velocity and \(\dfdx{x}{t}\) \(\left(\dot{x}=\right)\) is a horizontal velocity. In parametric form the slope of the line tangent to our curve is still the differential ratio of \(\dx{y}\) and \(\dx{x}\text{.}\) Similarly the area under our curve is still given by \(\int{}y\dx{x} \text{.}\) In our example of the falling ball this is
    \begin{equation*} \int{}y\dx{x} =\int{}4.9t^2\dx{t} \text{.} \end{equation*}
    So if we wanted to compute the area of the region beneath the curve \(P(t)= \ParamEqTwo{x(t)}{y(t)}=\ParamEqTwo{t}{-4.9t^2}\) we would compute
    \begin{equation} \int_{t_1}^{t_2}4.9t^2\dx{t} \tag{3.1} \end{equation}
    where \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) are the times at the beginning and end of the ball’s flight, respectively.
    🔗
    But that example is too simple because of the nature of the parameterization — in particular the fact that \(y(t)=t\text{.}\) To get a feel for integration in a parametric setting we will begin with two slightly more substantial problems: The area of a circle with radius \(=a\text{,}\) and the Area Under a Cycloid Generated From a Circle With Radius \(= a\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 3.1.0.1. The Area of a Circle With Radius \(\boldsymbol{= a}\).

    A circle with radius \(a\) can be parametrized by:
    \begin{equation*} P(\theta)= \ParamEqTwo {x(\theta)} {y(\theta)}= \ParamEqTwo {a\cos(\theta))} {a\sin(\theta)} \end{equation*}
    where \(0\le{}\theta{}\lt{}2\pi{}\) so the area of the circle is given by
    \begin{gather*} \int{}y\dx{x} = \int{}\underbrace{a\sin\left(\theta{}\right)}_{y(\theta{})}\underbrace{\left(-a\sin(\theta)\right) \dx{\theta}}_{\dx{x}} = a^2\int{}\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}, \end{gather*}
    once we find appropriate indices. Unfortunately this will not always be a simple matter. Indeed, you will find that the hardest part of the integration of parametric functions is often just finding the right indices. Let’s see why.
    🔗
    At first its seems that this integral is relatively simple: Simply integrate from right to left as usual. This gives
    \begin{gather*} a^2\int_{x=-a}^a\sin^2(\theta)\dx{\theta } \end{gather*}
    which has several problems.
    🔗
    Notice that the integration variable is \(\theta\) not \(x\text{.}\) To be sure we’ve seen integrals where the indices did not match the integration variable before, but we always arranged for the indices and the integration variable to match before proceeding to compute the integral. We will need for them to match here too.
    🔗
    One option is to re–express \(\theta{}\) in terms of \(x\text{.}\) That will work but it is an unattractive choice since it requires that invert the function \(x(\theta{})\text{.}\)
    🔗
    A much more attractive choice is to find \(\theta{}\) such that
    \begin{gather*} x(\theta{}) = a\cos(\theta{}) = -a. \end{gather*}
    Solving for \(\theta{}\) we find that \(\theta{}=\pi{}\text{.}\) Similarly when \(x(\theta{})=a\text{,}\) \(\theta{}=0\) so it appears that we need to compute
    \begin{gather} a^2\int_{\theta =\pi{}}^0\sin^2(\theta )\dx{\theta }\tag{3.2} \end{gather}
    but this is still inadequate since the value of this integral is zero, which is obviously wrong. Try it and see.
    🔗
    So what went wrong?
    🔗
    We began by thinking of integration as “summing up the areas of rectangles” because it is an intuitive and attractive starting point. But in much the same way that not every derivative is a slope not every integral is the sum of the areas of rectangles.
    🔗
    Look closely at equation (3.2). We can certainly interpret the differentials \(\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}\) as “rectangles with infinitesimal width” but will summing their areas give us the area of the circle. Are they even inside the the circle? Do you see anything that you can interpret as “differential rectangles filling the circle?” Clearly not.
    🔗
    This is puzzling but the problem lies in our “way of thinking” about integration. It is too narrow, too closely tied to the idea of summing the areas of rectangles. We need to expand it a bit.
