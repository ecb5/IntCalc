    Section 4.6 Center of Mass

    In Problem 4.4.0.6 and Problem 4.5.0.14, we made reference to a theorem of Pappus. That theorem says that if a region is revolved about a line not passing through the region, then the volume of the solid of revolution is equal to the area of the region times the circumference of the circle formed by revolving the center of mass (centroid) of this region about the line. Another theorem of Pappus states that the surface area of a surface formed by revolving a curve about a line is equal to the length of the curve times the circumference of the circle formed by revolving the centroid of the curve about the line.
    🔗
    This played out in the two PIC’s as the centroids of a disk and its boundary circle are both the center of the circle.
    🔗
    Disks and circles are pretty straightforward, but what about finding the center of mass of a general region. From a physical point of view, this region can represent a thin plate whose density could vary. To keep the problem geometric (and simpler), we will assume that the density is constantly 1 so that mass really is area. In this case, the center of mass is what we have been calling the centroid of the region.
    🔗
    Everyone probably has heard the term “center of mass.” But what does this mean? Let’s start this with something that everyone is familiar with: an average of grades. If we have five grades: \(96\text{,}\) \(87\text{,}\) \(92\text{,}\) \(83\text{,}\) \(90\text{,}\) then all students know that the average, denoted \(\bar{X}{}\) (read \(X\) bar) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \bar{X} =\frac{96+87+92+83+90}{5}=89.6 \end{equation*}
    What does this average mean? This means that this student’s overall performance is equivalent to the performance of a student who earned 89.6 on each of the five tests. It does not take into account fluctuations among the grades, but gives an overall measure. To put this in a more physical light, if we considered a system of points each with mass \(1\) at these locations \(96\text{,}\) \(87\text{,}\) \(92\text{,}\) \(83\text{,}\) \(90\) on a number line, then collectively, this system behaves the same as a mass of \(5\) located at the point \(89.6\text{.}\) It doesn’t take into account the relationships among the various grades; as much as in astronomy where there is a tendency to “shrink” the mass of a planet to its center of mass, with disregard to the dynamics in that planet.
    🔗
    In general, if we placed a point mass of \(1\) at each of the positions \(x_1\text{,}\) \(x_2\text{,}\) \(x_3\text{,}\) \(\cdots \text{,}\) \(x_n\text{,}\) then the center of mass \(\bar{x} \text{,}\) should satisfy the relationship
    \begin{align*} \bar{x} \amp = \frac{x_1\cdot 1+x_2\cdot 1+\cdots x_n\cdot 1}{n} \end{align*}
    To help with our discussions on this, we will give a name to the numerator. People have christened this the (first) moment of the system (about the origin). To give it a simple way to remember it, we write
    \begin{align*} \text{moment}\amp =\text{position}\cdot \text{mass}\\ \text{ center of mass }\amp =\frac{\text{total moment}}{\text{mass}} \end{align*}
    Later we will have use for the second moment about the origin, namely
    \begin{equation*} 2^\text{nd} \text{ moment} = (\text{position})^2\cdot \text{mass} \end{equation*}
    In general, one could look at the \(n^\text{th}\) moment as
    \begin{equation*} n^\text{th} \text{ moment } = (\text{position}{})^n\cdot \text{mass} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let’s apply this reasoning and our integration strategy to an infinite number of points. For example, the interval \([a,b]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In this case, our simple formula doesn’t hold as the position varies throughout the interval. Thus we apply our integration strategy. That is, we divide the interval into infinitely small intervals where the simple formula applies. The simple formula for moment applies to this small subinterval, so we have
    \begin{equation*} \text{moment of subinterval }= \text{position}\cdot\text{mass}=x\dx{x}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Integrating these individual moments gives us that the total moment of the interval \([a,b]\) is
    \begin{equation*} \text{total moment }=\int_{x=a}^{b}x\dx{x} =\eval{\left( \frac{x^2}{2}\right)}{x}{a}^b=\frac{b^2-a^2}{2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Thus, we would have
    \begin{equation*} \text{center of mass }=\frac{\text{total moment}}{\text{mass}}=\frac{b^2-a^2}{2(b-a)}=\frac{a+b}{2}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    This agrees with your intuitive notion that the center of mass of the interval should be located at the midpoint of the interval.
    🔗

    Problem 4.6.0.3.

    Find the center of mass of the set \(A=[a_1,b_1]\cup [a_2, b_2]\text{,}\) \(b_1\lt a_2\text{.}\) Is the center of mass of the set \(A\) equal to the averages of the midpoints of the two intervals? Does this make sense?
    🔗
    🔗
    We did not want to give it away while you were doing the previous problem, but you will actually get what is known as a weighted average; that is, the center of mass of the two midpoints where the mass of each interval is condensed at its midpoint.
    🔗
    What if we ramp this up to two-dimensions. Suppose we have a number of points of mass \(1\) located at \((x_1, y_1), (x_2, y_2), \cdots (x_n, y_n)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By the same reasoning as before, the center of mass \(\bar{X} \) should have the property that this system as a whole should behave the same as if the entire mass \(m\) was located at \(\bar{X} \text{.}\) In other words, we should have
    \begin{equation*} n\cdot \bar{X} = (x_1, y_1)\cdot{1}+(x_2, y_2)\cdot{1}+\cdots (x_n, y_n)\cdot{1} \end{equation*}
    This leads to the formulation
    \begin{equation*} \bar{X} =\left(\frac{x_1+x_2+\cdots +x_n}{n}, \frac{y_1+y_2+\cdots +y_n}{n}\right) = (\bar{x}, \bar{y})\text{.} \end{equation*}
    The thing to take away from this is that even though the center of mass, has two coordinates, we treat each coordinate separately and they are the averages of the \(x\) coordinates and \(y\) coordinates, respectively. Also, each coordinate still fits into the formula \(\frac{\text{total moment}}{\text{mass} }\text{.}\) Let’s use this same idea to a curve of uniform density \(1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Using analogous reasoning, the center of mass of this curve will be given by \(\bar{X} =(\bar{x} , \bar{y} )\) where each coordinate fits into our scheme \(\frac{\text{total moment}}{\text{mass} }\text{.}\) . To this end, we will look at the mass of the curve. Since the density is constantly \(1\text{,}\) the mass is really the arc length and is given by \(\int_{X=A}^B\dx{s}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To address \(\bar{x} \text{,}\) we will compute the moment of the curve about the line \(x=0\text{.}\) Dividing our curve into infinitely small pieces, we can use our simple formulation.
    🔗

    Problem 4.6.0.7.

    Provide the analogous reasoning (and diagrams) to show that the total moment about the line \(y=0\) on the interval \([A,B]\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \int_{X=A}^B y\dx{s}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Putting all of this together we have
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\int_{X=A}^B x\dx{s}}{\int_{X=A}^B \dx{s}}, \text{ and } \bar{y} = \frac{\int_{X=A}^B y\dx{s}}{\int_{X=A}^B \dx{s}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Again, we urge you to go through this process rather than trying to memorize these formulas.
    🔗

    To obtain Pappus’ theorem for volumes we need to expand this idea to the center of mass of a two-dimensional region. Again, the center of mass \(\bar{X} \) can be split into two coordinates \((\bar{x}, \bar{y} )\)) which represent the averages of the \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates of all of the points in the region.
    🔗
    With this in mind, consider a region \(R\) in the \(xy\) plane which can be thought of as two-dimensional plate with constant density \(1\text{.}\) We will draw this region shortly.
    🔗
    Again, we will use our strategy of dividing our region into pieces where a simple formula can be applied, and we will apply our general idea that the centroid is given by the formula \(\frac{\text{sum of location mass}}{\text{total mass}}\) The mass is just the area, so we will focus on the numerator.
    🔗
    Specifically, if we want to find \(\bar{x} \) , we will draw a generic vertical box and determine its moment about the line \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The moment of this box is straightforward as every point in it is the same distance \((x)\) from the \(y\) axis. Formally, we have
    \begin{align*} \text{moment of box (about} x=0) \amp = (\text{position of box})\cdot (\text{mass of box})\\ \amp =x\cdot (y_2-y_1)\dx{x} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Integrating these moments, we get that the total moment of the region is given by
    \begin{equation*} \text{total moment}=\int_{x=a}^c x(y_2-y_1)\dx{x}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    Thus, with this set up, we would have
    \begin{equation*} \bar{x} = \frac{\text{moment of }R}{\text{mass of }R}=\frac{\int_{x=a}^c x(y_2-y_1)\dx{x}}{\int_{x=a}^c (y_2-y_1)\dx{x}}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As before, you could memorize the formulas, but it is much better to remember simple concepts such as \(\text{moment } = \text{mass}\cdot \text{position}\) and apply these to a situation where things are constant. For example, we can apply the same reasoning to a horizontal box as below to derive the formula for \(\bar{y}\text{.}\) This is what integration is all about.
    🔗

    Problem 4.6.0.13.

    Use a horizontal box in the above diagram (appropriately labeled) to show that \(\bar{y}\) for the region \(R\) is given by
    \begin{equation*} \bar{y}= \frac{\text{moment of }R}{\text{mass of }R}=\frac{\int_{y=a}^c y(x_2-x_1)\dx{y}}{\int_{y=a}^c (y_2-y_1)\dx{y}} \end{equation*}

    Problem 4.6.0.14.

    🔗

    (a)

    In everything we did so far, we assumed that the region had a constant density and we said the density was \(1\text{.}\) Would it have made a difference if we said the density was given by some other constant \(\delta \text{?}\) Explain.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Suppose the density \(\delta \) was not constant. How would that alter the formulas for the center of mass?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Again, you can memorize formulas, but understanding them helps you to recreate them should you forget or more appropriately, adapt the ideas should the occasion necessitate that. For example, consider the region below bounded by \(y=\sin(x) \) and \(y=0\text{,}\) \(0\le x\le \pi \text{.}\)
    🔗
    Assuming the density is constant, it is straightforward to see that \(\bar{x}=\frac\pi2 \text{.}\) But what about \(\bar{y}\text{?}\) If we use the formula you derived above, we would have
    \begin{equation*} \bar{y}=\bar{y}= \frac{\text{moment of }R}{\text{mass of }R}=\frac{\int_{y=a}^c y(x_2-x_1)\dx{y}}{\int_{y=a}^c (y_2-y_1)\dx{y}} \end{equation*}
    Getting everything in terms of \(y\) would be problematic (but doable). It would be nice to keep everything in terms of \(x\) which would entail using a vertical box. For the area in the denominator, this is not a problem and in fact is probably how you would have computed the area to begin with. For the numerator, let’s put in our vertical box and see if we can compute its moment about the line \(x=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The problem with computing the moment (about \(x=0\)) of this vertical box is that our simple formula \(\text{mass}\cdot \text{position}\) won’t work as the position varies throughout the box. But wait a minute! We know what \(\bar{y}\) for this box is, namely \(\frac{y}{2}\text{.}\) We also know what area of this box is, namely \(y\dx{y}\text{.}\) Using that
    \begin{equation*} \frac{y}{2}=\bar{y}_{\text{box}}=\frac{\text{moment of box}}{\text{area of box}} \end{equation*}
    so that
    \begin{equation*} \text{moment of box}=\left(\frac{y}{2}\right)(y\dx{x})=\frac{y^2}{2}\dx{x}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
