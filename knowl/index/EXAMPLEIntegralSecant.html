Exploration 2.1.2.1. The Integral of an Arbitrary Polynomial in \(\sec(x) \) and \(\tan{x}\).

As we’ve just seen once integration by substitution has been mastered finding the integral of \(\tan (x)\) is straightforward. Based on this experience it would seem reasonable to assume that \(\int \sec (x)\dx{x}\) would be equally straightforward.
🔗
And it is, sort of. We just need to make the right substitution but what that substitution might be is far from obvious.
🔗
We’ll use the substitution \(u=\tan (x) +\sec(x) \text{.}\) You almost certainly don’t see how this will work, but that is part of the point we want to make with this example. You are free to make any substitution you like, and sometimes the right substitution is not obvious. The only way to find out if a given substitution will work is to try it and see.
🔗
(a)
Use the substitution \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x)\) to show that
\begin{equation*} \int\sec (x)\dx{x} =\int \frac{\dx{u}}{u} =\ln\abs{u} +C = \ln\abs{\sec (x) + \tan (x)} +C \end{equation*}
🔗
This integration is sometimes taught using the following “trick” which is equivalent to our development above:
\begin{align*} \int\sec(x)\dx{x} \amp{}= \int\sec(x)\underbrace{\frac{\tan (x)+\sec (x)}{\tan (x)+\sec (x)}}_{=1}\dx{x}. \end{align*}
Now let \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x) \) so that \(\dx{u} =\sec(x)\tan(x)+\sec^2(x) \) so that
\begin{align*} \amp{}=\int\frac1u\dx{u}\\ \amp{}=\ln\abs{u}+C\\ \amp{}=\ln\abs{\sec(x)+\tan(x)} +C \end{align*}
🔗
Clearly whoever first computed the value of this integral found it by some other means (possibly by guessing) and then realized that using the substitution \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x) \) would suffice, even if it is not very illuminative.
🔗
This digression is based on the paper Integrating Polynomials in Secant and Tangent, by Jonathan P. McCammond, The American Mathematical Monthly, Nov., 1999, Vol. 106, No. 9
🔗
We have called the substitution \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x) \) a trick but what seems to be a trick is often a special case of more general technique. This the situation here. We would like to find the underlying structure behind Exploration 2.1.2.1.
🔗
The technique follows from definitions
\begin{align} u\amp{}=\sec(x)+\tan(x)\tag{2.9}\\ v\amp{}=\sec(x)-\tan(x)\tag{2.10} \end{align}
from which we see that
\begin{align} \sec(x) \amp{}=\frac12 (u+v)\tag{2.11}\\ \tan(x) \amp{}=\frac12 (u-v).\tag{2.12} \end{align}
🔗
🔗
(b)
Use equation (2.11) and equation (2.12).
🔗
(i)
Show that
\begin{equation} uv=1\text{.}\tag{2.13} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
(ii)
Show that
\begin{equation} \sec(x)\dx{x} = \frac1u \dx{u} = - \frac1v \dx{v}. \tag{2.14} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
🔗
(c)
For our first example we will compute
\begin{align*} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x} \amp{}= \int \sec^2(x)\sec{x}\dx{x} \end{align*}
From equation (2.11) we have
\begin{align*} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x}\amp{}=\frac14\int(u+v)^2\sec(x)\dx{x}\\ \amp{}=\frac14\int(u^2+2uv+v^2)\sec(x)\dx{x} \end{align*}
and from equation (2.13) we have
\begin{align*} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x}=\amp{}\frac14\int(u^2+2+v^2))\sec(x)\dx{x}\\ =\amp{}\frac14\int u^2\left(\frac{1}{u}\dx{u}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int\sec(x)\dx{x} -\frac14 \int v^2\left(\frac{1}{v}\dx{v}\right)\\ =\amp{}\frac18\left(u^2-v^2\right)+\frac12\int\sec(x)\dx{x}\\ =\amp{}\frac18\left[\left(\sec(x)+\tan(x)\right)^2-\left( \sec(x)-\tan(x)\right)^2 \right]\\ \amp{}+\frac12\ln{\abs{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}}+C \end{align*}
So finally,
\begin{equation} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x}=\frac12\left(\sec(x)\tan(x)+\ln{\abs{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}}\right)+C.\tag{2.15} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
Do you notice anything interesting about equation (2.15)? Look closely. It says that the integral of \(\sec^3(x) \) is the average of the derivative of \(\sec(x) \) and the integral of \(\sec(x) \text{.}\)
🔗
Prof. Rick Poss of Norbert College found noticed this fact and found it sufficiently interesting that he and his colleague John Frohliger decided to determine if there were other functions with this property. The results of their investigations were published in Mathematics Magazine, Vol. 62, #4, October 1989 with the title Just an Average Integral
