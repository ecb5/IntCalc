Exploration 2.1.2.1. The Integral of an Arbitrary Polynomial in \(\boldsymbol{\sec(x)} \) and \(\boldsymbol{\tan{(x)}}\).

Once integration by substitution has been mastered computing \(\int \tan (x)\dx{x}\) is straightforward as we’ve just seen. Based on this experience it would seem reasonable to assume that \(\int \sec (x)\dx{x}\) would be equally straightforward. And it is. Sort of. We just need to find the right substitution. But what that might be is far from obvious.
🔗
(a) The Integral of the Secant.
Use the substitution \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x)\) to show that
\begin{equation} \int\sec (x)\dx{x} =\int \frac{\dx{u}}{u} =\ln\abs{u} +C = \ln\abs{\sec (x) + \tan (x)} +C\tag{2.9} \end{equation}
🔗
Clearly whoever first computed the value of this integral found it by some other means (possibly by guessing) and then realized that using the substitution \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x) \) would suffice, even if it is not very illuminative.
🔗
🔗
(b)
Use the substitution \(u=\cot(x)+\csc(x)\) to show that
\begin{equation} \int\csc (x)\dx{x} = \ln\abs{\csc (x) + \cot (x)} +C\tag{2.10} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
(c)
We have called the substitution \(u=\tan(x)+\sec(x)\) a trick but what seems to be a trick is often a special case of more general technique. This the situation here. We would like to find the underlying structure behind equation (2.9) and (2.10).
🔗
The rest of this exploration is based on the paper Integrating Polynomials in Secant and Tangent, by Jonathan P. McCammond, The American Mathematical Monthly, Nov., 1999, Vol. 106, No. 9
🔗
The technique follows from definitions
\begin{align} u\amp{}=\sec(x)+\tan(x)\tag{2.11}\\ v\amp{}=\sec(x)-\tan(x)\tag{2.12} \end{align}
from which we see that
\begin{align} \sec(x) \amp{}=\frac12 (u+v)\tag{2.13}\\ \tan(x) \amp{}=\frac12 (u-v).\tag{2.14} \end{align}
🔗
🔗
(d)
Use equation (2.13) and equation (2.14).
🔗
(i)
Show that
\begin{equation} uv=1\text{.}\tag{2.15} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
(ii)
Show that
\begin{equation} \sec(x)\dx{x} = \frac1u \dx{u} = - \frac1v \dx{v}. \tag{2.16} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
🔗
(e)
For our first example we will compute
\begin{align*} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x} \amp{}= \int \sec^2(x)\sec{x}\dx{x} \end{align*}
From equation (2.13) we have
\begin{align*} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x}\amp{}=\frac14\int(u+v)^2\sec(x)\dx{x}\\ \amp{}=\frac14\int(u^2+2uv+v^2)\sec(x)\dx{x} \end{align*}
and from equation (2.15) we have
\begin{align*} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x}=\amp{}\frac14\int(u^2+2+v^2))\sec(x)\dx{x}\\ =\amp{}\frac14\int u^2\left(\frac{1}{u}\dx{u}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\int\sec(x)\dx{x} -\frac14 \int v^2\left(\frac{1}{v}\dx{v}\right)\\ =\amp{}\frac18\left(u^2-v^2\right)+\frac12\int\sec(x)\dx{x}\\ =\amp{}\frac18\left[\left(\sec(x)+\tan(x)\right)^2-\left( \sec(x)-\tan(x)\right)^2 \right]\\ \amp{}+\frac12\ln{\abs{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}}+C \end{align*}
So finally,
\begin{equation} \int\sec^3(x)\dx{x}=\frac12\left(\sec(x)\tan(x)+\ln{\abs{\sec(x)+\tan(x)}}\right)+C.\tag{2.17} \end{equation}
🔗
🔗
Do you notice anything interesting about equation (2.17)? Look closely. It says that the integral of \(\sec^3(x) \) is the average of the derivative of \(\sec(x) \) and the integral of \(\sec(x) \text{.}\)
🔗
Prof. Rick Poss of Norbert College found noticed this fact and found it sufficiently interesting that he and his colleague John Frohliger decided to determine if there were other functions with this property. The results of their investigations were published in Mathematics Magazine, Vol. 62, #4, October 1989 with the title Just an Average Integral
