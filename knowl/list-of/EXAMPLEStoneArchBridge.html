Example 20.2.0.12 A Stone Arch Bridge.

When we first encountered the catenary curve in Section 5.10 we mentioned that masons had discovered—mostly from centuries of experience by trial and error—that domes and arches built in the form an inverted catenary were very stable. We’d like to reach the same conclusion using Calculus.
🔗
Consider one half of a stone arch bridge drawn below. In Figure 20.2.0.14 the positive \(y\)-axis points downward. As in Section 5.10 we will focus on the forces at a generic point \(P\) with coordinates \((x,y)\text{.}\)
🔗
The problem is to find a formula for the curve \({y=y(x)}\) with the property that the vertical component of the tangential force \(T\) at \(P\) is equal to the weight of the bridge from \(0\) to \(x\text{,}\) and the horizontal force along the length of the bridge at \(P\) is constant (denoted \(H\)) . We let \(w\) denote the weight density of the stone (per cross sectional area). If \(A(x)\) denotes the area of the section of the bridge between \(0\) and \(x\) then \(wA(x)\) is the weight of the bridge at the point \(P=(x,y) \text{.}\) We thus have the following representation of the forces at \(P\text{.}\)
🔗
Thus the slope of \(T\) \(P\) is equaling \(\frac{wA}{H}\text{,}\) so we get the differential equation
\begin{equation*} \dfdx{y}{x}=\frac{w}{H}A \end{equation*}
🔗
The problem here is that we don’t know what \(A\) is. However, a moment’s thought tells us that we do know what the differential \(\dx{A}\) is. If we increase \(x\) to \(x+\dx{x}\text{,}\) then we can see that \(\dx{A}=y\dx{x}\)
🔗
Thus, by differentiating the above equation we see that the arch should satisfy the differential equation
\begin{equation*} \dfdxn{y}{x}{2}=\frac{w}{H}\dfdx{A}{x}=\frac{w}{H} y\text{.} \end{equation*}
