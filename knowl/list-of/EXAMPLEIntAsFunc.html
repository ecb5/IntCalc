Example 3.2.0.1. The Integral as a Function.

Suppose we drive due west on a straight, flat road at \(60\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}\text{.}\) At time \(t=a\) we pass through a city as in Figure 3.2.0.2. How far will we be from the city at time \(t=\tau{}\gt{a}\text{?}\)
🔗
Before we continue note that you already know the solution to this problem. It will be sixty miles per hour times the length of time spent driving between the city and our position at time \(\tau \text{.}\) The point here is not to find the solution but to use the ideas and notation of Calculus to do so. Once we’ve used Calculus to solve problems we already understand we are better positioned to use in in an unfamiliar setting.
🔗
During every infinitesimal instant \(\dx{t}\text{,}\) we travel the infinitesimal distance \(60\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}\times \dx{t} \text{ hours} \text{.}\) Summing these we see that the total distance is represented by:
\begin{gather*} \int 60\dx{t} \text{ miles}. \end{gather*}
The problem, of course, is that the distance traveled depends uniquely on (is an ordinary function of) the elapsed time, but we’ve already defined the symbol \(\int 60\dx{t}\) to be the most general antiderivative (a multifunction). We’ll need to modify our notation a bit to distinguish our current problem from the general antiderivative problem.
🔗
Since we arrive at the city at time \(t=a\) we must have begun somewhere east of the city at time \(t=0\text{.}\) Ignoring the arbitrary constant for the moment we see that the distance from our starting point to the city (when \(t=a\)) is
\begin{gather*} \eval{\int{60}\dx{t}}{t}{a} \end{gather*}
whereas the distance from the starting point to our location at time \(t=\tau{}\) is
\begin{gather*} \eval{\int{60}\dx{t}}{t}{\tau{}} \end{gather*}
Clearly then the distance from the city to our position at time \(t=\tau{}\) is
\begin{gather} D(\tau)= \eval{\int{60}\dx{t}}{t}{\tau{}} - \eval{\int{60}\dx{t}}{t}{a}.\tag{3.4} \end{gather}
🔗
Finally, notice how much notation is repeated in equation (3.4). To avoid having to write all of that every time we do this sort of computation we will combine the two terms as follows:
\begin{gather*} \eval{\int{60}\dx{t}}{t}{\tau{}} - \eval{\int{60}\dx{t}}{t}{a} = \tbeval{\int 60\dx{t}}{t=a}{\tau{}}. \end{gather*}
So to compute our distance from the city at time \(t=\tau{}\) we need to evaluate
\begin{gather*} D(\tau)= \tbeval{\int 60\dx{t}}{t=a}{\tau{}}. \end{gather*}
This is accomplished by finding any antiderivative of the integrand and computing the difference between it’s value at \(t=a\) and \(t=\tau \text{.}\) One antiderivative of \(60\) is \(60t+C\) so
\begin{align*} D(\tau)\amp{} = \tbeval{\int 60\dx{t}}{t=a}{\tau{}}\\ \amp{}=\tbeval{ 60t +C}{t=a}{\tau{}}\\ \amp{}= \left(60\tau +C\right) - \left(60a +C\right)\\ \amp{}=60(\tau-a) \end{align*}
or sixty miles per hour times the length of time we drive, as we stated at the beginning.
🔗
Notice that our earlier decision to ignore the arbitrary constant is justified by this last computation since the constant will alway subtract out.
