How Archimedes Did It.

Archimedes of Syracuse established himself as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time in part due to his innovative and dextrous use of the Method of Exhaustion to compute a diverse set of areas and volumes. His computation of the area of general Parabolic Section in particular anticipates both the definite integral and infinite series (see Chapter 5).
🔗
Archimedes was able to show the results of Problem 4.3.0.6 but his methods were considerably more laborious for two reasons.
🔗
First, he obtained the result for a completely general parabola. In Problem 4.3.0.6 we only showed the result for the parabola with equation \(y=x^2\text{.}\) To generalize our result would have required another major computation and would take us to far afield for demonstration purposes.
🔗
Second, Archimedes did not have access to algebraic notation of any kind. Thus all of the results we obtained by manipulating formulas algebraically he would have had to interpret geometrically, which is a considerably more cumberson endeavor.
🔗
But, having obtained the results in part (a) of Problem 4.3.0.6, he was able to obtain part (c) directly as follows.
🔗
Notice that in Figure 4.3.0.7 the lines \(\overline{AC}\) and \(\overline{BC}\) each cut off another section of the parabola.
🔗
Next he showed that
\begin{equation*} \text{area of }\triangle ACF + \triangle CBE =\frac{1}{4}\text{(area of } \triangle ABC). \end{equation*}
The line segments \(\overline{AF}\text{,}\) \(\overline{FC}\text{,}\) \(\overline{CE}\text{,}\) and \(\overline{EB}\) define four more parabolic sections. Repeating his process Archimedes was able to show (essentially) that the area of the original parabolic section is the sum:
\begin{equation*} \triangle ABC\left[1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \right] \end{equation*}
which he was able to show is equal to
\begin{equation*} \triangle ABC\cdot\frac{4}{3}. \end{equation*}
🔗
We will return to this in [provisional cross-reference: Infinite Series] where we will develop a method for computing the sum
\begin{equation*} 1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \end{equation*}
We will also extend our method to more general infinite sums.
