(c)

Let’s complicate things a bit further. Suppose we have
\begin{equation*} \int\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-6x}}\dx{x}. \end{equation*}
🔗
Once again our strategy is to manipulate the radicand
 4 
The part under the radical.
so that it looks like
\begin{equation*} \text{something}^2-1. \end{equation*}
Completing the square will work:
\begin{align*} x^2-6x \amp{}= x^2-6x+9-9\\ \amp{}=(x-3)^2-9\\ \amp{}=9\left( \left(\frac{x-3}{3}\right)^2 -1 \right). \end{align*}
Thus
\begin{equation*} \int\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-6x}}\dx{x} = \frac{1}{3}\int\frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(\frac{x-3}{3}\right)^2-1}}\dx{x}. \end{equation*}
🔗
Use the substitution \(\frac{x-3}{3}=\sec\left(\theta \right)\) to show that
\begin{equation*} \int\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-6x}}\dx{x} =\ln\left(x-3+\sqrt{x^2-6x}\right)+C \end{equation*}
