Example 21.2.0.3.

(a)

We want to find the area of the parabolic section defined by the graphs of
\begin{align*} y\amp{}=x^2 \text{ and }\\ y\amp{}=2x. \end{align*}
🔗
The first step is always to sketch the problem. In this case we only need to graph our two curves.
🔗
We want to “exhaust” the area of the section by filling it with infinitely many differential rectangles, like the generic rectangle in red, of width \(\dx{x}\text{.}\) Since the upper and lower endpoints of our rectangle lie on the upper and lower bounding curves respectively, we see that the area of a typical rectangle is
\begin{equation*} \text{Area of rectangle} = \left(2x-x^2\right)\dx{x.} \end{equation*}
🔗
This is the set up. Notice that by labeling the sketch clearly, we were able to easily read the area of the rectangle right off the picture. This was the point — to divide the problem into easily computable parts.
🔗
To compute the area of the shaded region we need to add these “differential rectangles” together using the FTC.
\begin{equation*} \text{Total Area of the Section} = \int_{x=0}^{2}\left(2x-x^2\right)\dx{x}=\frac{4}{3}. \end{equation*}
🔗
We highly recommend that you follow this pattern closely whenever you need to compute a definite integral. You want to “exhaust” the area you are interested in by filling it with figures whose area is known. Most of the time these will be rectangles. But whatever they are your first task is to generate a generic formula for their area. So focus your attention first on the local problem. Then sum the differential areas using the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
🔗
Follow this routine on every problem, no matter how easy it may seem. This will help you to develop a habit of careful analysis. That habit will serve you well as the applications get more complicated. We (the authors) would insist but obviously we have no way to enforce the injunction. Nevertheless, we know what we are talking about. Listen to us.
🔗
🔗

(b)

In part (a) we divided the \(x-\)axis into differentials which gave our red “differential rectangles” a vertical orientation. But of course there is nothing special about that choice. We could also have divided the \(y-\)axis into differentials which would have resulted in rectangles with a horizontal orientation. Either way we should obtain the same area. What is important is that we divide the region consistently into “parallel” rectangles. (Recall Cavalieri’s “all the lines.” Our differential rectangles are essentially taking on the role of Cavalieri’s parallel lines.)
🔗
The appropriate sketch reflecting that choice is shown in Figure 21.2.0.5. Notice that this time the (horizontally oriented) rectangles will be stacked vertically like a pile of books on a table, rather than horizontally like books on a shelf. This means that our summation will be oriented vertically this time. Put more simple the summation variable is \(y\) when the differentials are \(\dx{y}\text{,}\) and \(x\) when the differentials are \(\dx{x}\text{.}\) You cannot use Theorem 20.2.0.3 to compute an integral if the variable in the integrand matches the variable in the differential. This knowledge can serve as a check to make sure you have set up the definite integral correctly.
🔗
Continuing, we need an expression for the area of our red rectangle. Do you see what it is? Give it some thought before reading on.
🔗
The left end of the rectangle is on the curve \(y=2x\) and the right end is on the curve \(y=x^2\text{.}\) The length of our rectangle will be the difference between the \(x-\)coordinates of these points. That is the area of our rectangle is
\begin{equation*} \left(\sqrt{y}-\frac{y}{2}\right)\dx{y} \end{equation*}
Since we are summing from bottom-to-top this time (rather than left-to-right) we need to use the \(y-\)coordinates in the indices. Thus our definite integral is:
\begin{equation*} \text{Total Area of the Section} = \int_{y=0}^{4} \left(\sqrt{y}-\frac{y}{2}\right)\dx{y} = \frac{4}{3}. \end{equation*}
Since the area of the section has not changed we expect to get the same number however we compute it.
🔗
