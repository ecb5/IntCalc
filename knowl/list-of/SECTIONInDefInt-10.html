The Definite Integral.

In part (c) of Example 20.2.0.1 we saw that we can compute the area under the graph of the curve \(f(t)=t-\frac{t^2}{100}\) by defining the accumulation function
\begin{gather*} F(x) =\int_{t=0}^{x}f(t)\dx{t} \end{gather*}
and evaluating it at \(x=100\text{.}\)
🔗
If we only need to find the area between \(0\) and \(50\) we would simply stop accumulating the area at \(x=50\text{.}\) Thus evaluating
\begin{gather*} F(50) =\int_{t=0}^{50}f(t)\dx{t} \end{gather*}
would tell us that area.
🔗
Similarly if we only want the area between \(x=25\) and \(x=75\) we could write down the solution of the IVP
\begin{align*} \dfdx{G}{x}=f(x),\amp{}\amp{} G(25)=0 \end{align*}
and evaluate it at \(x=75\)
\begin{gather*} G(75)= \int_{t=25}^{75}f(t)\dx{t}=\int_{t=25}^{75}f(t)\dx{t}. \end{gather*}
🔗
We will often only be interested in the value of our accumulation function at a single point. In that case we will forego writing down the function and proceed immediately to evaluating \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(t)\dx{t}\) where \(a\) and \(b\) are the locations where we begin and end accumulating the differentials. Because in that case there is no confusion over the variables, dummy or otherwise, we will usually revert to our lifelong habit of using \(x\) as the variable. Because it has a definite numerical value the integral \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(x)\dx{x}\) is called a Definite Integral.
