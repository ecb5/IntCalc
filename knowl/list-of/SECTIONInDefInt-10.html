Three Distinct Uses of the Integral Symbol.

In part (b) of Example 20.2.0.1 we saw that we can compute the area under the graph of the curve \(f(t)=t-\frac{t^2}{100}\) by defining the accumulation function
\begin{gather*} F(x) =\int_{t=0}^{x}f(t)\dx{t} \end{gather*}
and evaluating it at \(x=100\text{.}\)
🔗
But if we only wanted to find the are between \(0\) and \(50\) we would simply stop accumulating the area at \(x=50\text{.}\) Evaluating
\begin{gather*} F(50) =\int_{t=0}^{50}f(t)\dx{t} \end{gather*}
would tell us that area.
🔗
Similarly if we only want the area between \(x=25\) and \(x=75\) we could write down the solution of the IVP
\begin{align*} \dfdx{G}{x}=f(x),\amp{}\amp{} G(25)=0 \end{align*}
and evaluate it at \(x=75\)
\begin{gather*} G(75)= \int_{t=25}^{75}f(t)\dx{t}=\int_{t=25}^{75}f(t)\dx{t}. \end{gather*}
🔗
We will often only be interested in the value of our accumulation function at a single point. In that case we will forego writing down the function an proceed immediately to evaluating \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(t)\dx{t}\) where \(a\) and \(b\) are the location points where we begin and end accumulating the differentials. Because there is no confusion over the variables, dummy or otherwise, in that case we will usually revert to our lifelong habit of using \(x\) as the variable:
\begin{gather*} \int_{x=a}^{b}f(x)\dx{x}. \end{gather*}
This is very unfair to the beginning student because it is unnecessarily confusing but it is also standard practice so it must be dealt with.
🔗
We now have three distinct situations where the integral symbol \(\left(\int\right)\) is used and each usage is distinct from the others, although they are related.
Antiderivatives:
🔗
We defined the notation \(\int f(x)\dx{x}\) to be a multifunction. Specifically, it is the most general antiderivative of \(f(x)\) and that definition still holds. As a practical matter for you it means that if \(F^\prime(x)=f(x) \) and you are asked to evaluate for example, \(\int f(x)\dx{x}\) the correct answer is
\begin{gather*} \int f(x)\dx{x}= F(x)+C \end{gather*}
not
\begin{gather*} \int f(x)\dx{x}= F(x). \end{gather*}
🔗
Accumulation Functions
🔗
The notation
\begin{gather*} \int_{t=a}^{x}f(t)\dx{t} \end{gather*}
represents an ordinary function, not a multifunction. Specifically it represents the accumulation function which sums differentials of the form \(f(t)\dx{t}\) starting at \(t=a\) and ending at \(t=x\text{.}\)
🔗
Both antiderivatives and accumulation functions are sometimes also called Indefinite Integrals, but we (the authors) will avoid using that nomenclature because we do not find it helpful to use a single term for two different concepts.
🔗
The Definite Integral
🔗
Finally, if \(a\) and \(b\) are constants then the notation \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(t)\dx{t}\) represents a number. Specifically it is the number that results from evaluating a certain accumulation function \(F(x)\) at \(x=b\text{,}\) but regardless of where the number comes from \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(t)\dx{t}\) represents a number, not a function, and not a multifunction.
🔗
Because it has a definite value the integral \(\int_{t=a}^{b}f(t)\dx{t}\) is called a Definite Integral.
