The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus was known to both Newton and Leibniz, and in some special cases to some of their predecessors. But Leibniz was the first to publish this result in the general form we see here. Leibniz’ works are very readable today because the notation he used proved to be so expressive that it is still in use today.
🔗
Gottfried Leibniz was not a professional mathematician or scientist. He received his doctorate in jurisprudence from the University of Altdorf in 1667 at the age of 21 and was promised a professorship there which he declined. Leibniz’ intellectual interests were broad, and he perceived universities as places which tended to stifle creative thought.
🔗
Leibniz was brilliant, but his training in mathematics at the time was rudimentary. In 1672, while on a diplomatic mission to Paris he met Christiann Huygens, one of most brilliant mathematicians and pre–eminent scientists at the time. Leibniz persuaded Huygens to take him under his wing. Leibniz credited much of his mathematical growth in Paris to Huygens. We have previously encountered Huygens in Subsection 6.2.3 and Section 6.8.
🔗
Before agreeing to tutor Leibniz, and in order to assess the younger man’s abilities, Huygens posed the following problem as a challenge: Find the sum of the reciprocals of the triangular numbers. That is, determine the sum
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{1}+\frac{1}{1+2}+\frac{1}{1+2+3}+\frac{1}{1+2+3+4}+\dots \end{equation*}
This problem had already been solved by Johann van Waveren Hudde whom we encountered in Problem 5.6.0.2, but Huygens was curious how the young Leibniz would approach it.
🔗
Using the known formula \(1+2+3+\dots +n=\frac{n\left(n+1\right)}{2}\text{,}\) the problem becomes
\begin{equation*} \frac{2}{1\cdot 2}+\frac{2}{2\cdot 3}+\frac{2}{3\cdot 4}+\dots \end{equation*}
Leibniz recognized that this could be rewritten as
\begin{equation*} 2\left[\left(\frac{1}{1} - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{4}\right) + \dots \right] \end{equation*}
and noted that nearly all of the fractions cancelled out leaving
\begin{equation*} 2\left(1-\frac{1}{\infty }\right)=2(1-0)=2. \end{equation*}
(In the seventeenth century it was commone to use the \(\infty\) symbol as if it were an actual number. But in modern parlance this usage is forbidden. Don’t do it. Your teacher will be horrified. )
🔗
It is not the result itself (which was known) but the technique which is important here. Later Leibniz surmised that the idea that the sum of differences is equal to the difference of the extremes would apply equally well to infinitely small differences (differentials). He published this idea in a short paper in 1693. Here is his idea.
🔗
Suppose we have
\begin{equation*} \dfdx{A}{x}=y \end{equation*}
so that
\begin{equation*} y\dx{x} =\dx{A} \end{equation*}
Integrating we have
\begin{equation*} \int^b_{x=a}{y\dx{x} }=\int^b_{x=a}{\dx{A} }=A\left(b\right)-A(a) \end{equation*}
🔗
We have used modern notation to highlight the association with definite integration. Leibniz’ explanation was much more wordy, and was accompanied by a very complicated diagram.
