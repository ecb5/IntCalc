Archimedes Quadrature of the Parabolic Section.

Archimedes of Syracuse established himself as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time due in part to his innovative and dextrous use of the Method of Exhaustion to compute a diverse set of areas and volumes. His computation of the area of general Parabolic Section in particular anticipates both the definite integral (see Chapter 21) and infinite series (see Chapter 22).
🔗

Definition 20.1.1.2. Parabolic Section.

A parabolic section is the region which a straight line cuts from a parabola. The shaded region in this sketch is a parabolic section.
🔗
🔗
Archimedes was able to compute the area of a parabolic section precisely using Eudoxus’ Method of Exhaustion. Because we are more interested in the underlying ideas we will not follow Archimedes’ argument in detail, but we will consider the broad outline since the method itself is fundamental and enlightening.
🔗
Archimedes began by inscribing the triangle \(\triangle ABC\) inside the sector as in Figure 20.1.1.3. Note that the point \(B\) is located horizontally halfway between points \(A\) and \(C\text{.}\)
🔗
Observing that the line segments \(\overline{AB}\) and \(\overline{BC}\) also cut off sections of the parabola, he then inscribed the triangles \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle BCE\) as in Figure 20.1.1.4. Archimedes was then able to show that
\begin{equation*} \text{area of }\triangle ABD + \triangle BCE =\frac{1}{4}\text{(area of } \triangle ABC). \text{.} \end{equation*}
🔗
Since the line segments \(\overline{AD}\text{,}\) \(\overline{DB}\text{,}\) \(\overline{BE}\text{,}\) and \(\overline{EC}\) define four more parabolic sections Archimedes continued repeating this process with progressivly smaller rectangles. He was able to show (essentially) that the area of the original parabolic section is:
\begin{equation*} \triangle ABC\left[1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \right] \end{equation*}
which he was able to show is equal to
\begin{equation*} \frac{4}{3}\left(\text{Area of }\triangle ABC\right). \end{equation*}
🔗
We will return to this in [provisional cross-reference: Infinite Series] where we will develop a method for computing the sum
\begin{equation*} 1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \end{equation*}
We will also extend our method to more general infinite sums.
