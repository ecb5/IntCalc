Archimedes Quadrature of the Parabolic Section.

Archimedes of Syracuse established himself as one of the greatest mathematicians of all time due in part to his innovative and dextrous use of the Method of Exhaustion to compute a diverse set of areas and volumes.
🔗
In this regard, a few of his accomplishments are as below.
  • Archimedes showed that the volume and surface area of a sphere is \(2/3\) that of the circumscribed cylinder. He was so proud of this particular result that he requested the symbol be carved in his tombstone after his death.
    🔗
    🔗
  • Archimedes showed that the area bounded by the first loop of a spiral and it initial line is equal to \(1/3\) that of the “first circle.” The picture below is more illuminating than our verbal description.
    🔗
    🔗
  • Given a parabolic segment with endpoints \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) draw a line through \(A\) parallel to the axis of the parabola hitting the tangent to the parabola at \(B\) at point \(C\text{.}\) Then the area of the parabolic segment is \(1/3\) the area of triangle \(ABC\text{.}\) Again, a picture is more illuminating.
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
Taking this step further, suppose \(D\) is the midpoint of \(AB\) and a line segment \(DE\) is drawn parallel to \(AC\) hitting the parabola at \(F\) and the line \(BC\) at \(E.\)
🔗
Then we shall see that the area of triangle \(ABC\) is four times that of triangle \(ABF\text{.}\) This says that the area of the parabolic segment is \(4/3\) that of the inscribed triangle \(ABF\text{.}\) Archimedes in fact proved this in an alternate way as well which we will explore when we cover the geometric series.

Notice that in each case, Archimedes is comparing two objects rather than giving an actual numerical answer for the area or volume. This is in part because of classical Greek scholarly tradition that was above actual computations which were more for tradesmen. This is actually where the term “quadrature of the parabola” comes in. It literally means to construct a square whose area is equal to that of the parabolic segment. When we say that a figure has an area of \(5^2\) square inches, it means that the figure has the same area as that of a \(5 \times{} 5\) square. Since the quadrature of a triangle was already well known at the time of Archimedes, then his result provided the quadrature of the parabolic segment. This comparison of objects is also a product of Archimedes’ geometric proof of these theorems. Basically, when given a statement comparing two objects, Archimedes showed that these must be equal because assuming one was bigger than the other would lead to a contradiction. But this begs the question, “How did Archimedes know which objects to compare?” In other words, Archimedes had to “know” what the answer was before he could prove it was correct. How did he discover these results? His method of discovery is revealed in the form of a letter (circa 250 BC) to Eratosthenes which describes his mechanical method for discovering areas and volumes. His approach is indicative of the fact that Archimedes was also an engineer and physicist and is a precursor to integration. He was also bold enough to embrace the use of infinitesimal quantities which were not understood enough to be utilized in formal geometric proofs. In ways, our approach to calculus mimics Archimedes as we utilized infinitesimals (differentials) to obtain results but then replaced these with limits to rigorously prove our results were correct. In Archimedes’ words,
🔗
“. . . I thought it might be appropriate to write down for you a special method, by means of which you will be able to recognize certain mathematical questions with the aid of mechanics. I am convinced that this is no less useful than finding the proofs of these same theorems . . . Some things, which first became clear to me by the mechanical method, were afterwards proved geometrically, because their investigation by that method does not furnish an actual demonstration. It is easier to supply the proof when we have previously acquired, by the method, some knowledge of the questions than it is to find it without any previous knowledge.”
🔗
🔗

🔗
Archimedes’ Method is to mentally slice objects into infinitely thin slices and balance these slices on a balance beam (lever). If the corresponding slices balance, then the objects must balance. As a simple example, consider the following diagram depicting a section of the parabola \(y=\sqrt{x}\) revolved around its axis (the solid is called a paraboloid) and a cylinder lying on a balance beam.
🔗
\begin{equation*} \text{area of slice } \cdot \text{ distance to fulcrum }= \pi {\left(\sqrt{x}\right)}^2\cdot b=\pi {\left(\sqrt{b}\right)}^2\cdot x \end{equation*}
Since the corresponding slices balance, then the solids themselves must balance. This gives us.
\begin{align*} \text{volume of paraboloid }\cdot b\amp{}=\text{ volume of cylinder} \cdot \frac{b}{2}\\ \text{volume of paraboloid }\amp{}=\frac{1}{2}\text{ volume of cylinder} =\frac{\pi }{2}b^2 \end{align*}
🔗
Let’s apply Archimedes’ Method to the area of the segment of a parabola above. To make things a bit easier, we will coordinatize things and consider the case where \(\)B is the vertex of the parabola. Here is the diagram.
🔗
\noindent Recall that in this diagram, \(AC\) is parallel to the axis of the parabola (the \(y\) axis) hitting the tangent line \(BC\) (the \(x\) axis) at the point \(C.\) The goal is to show that the area of the parabolic segment between \(A\ \)and \(B\) is 1/3 that of triangle \(ABC\text{.}\) To utilize Archimedes’ Method, we draw a lever \(BJ\) through the midpoint \(D\) of line segment \(AC\) with \(BD=DJ\text{.}\) If \(D\) acts as the fulcrum of the lever, we will show that if the slice \(EH\) of the parabola is placed with its center at \(J\) and the corresponding slice \(EG\) of the triangle is left where it is, then those segments will balance on the lever. That is, we will show that \(\left(EH\right)\left(DJ\right)=(EG)(ID)\text{.}\)

Since the corresponding slices balance, then by Archimedes’ Method, the parabolic segment should balance out with the triangle if the center of mass of the segment is placed at \(J\) and the triangle is left where it is. The only thing to do is locate the center of mass of the triangle. It turns out that the center of mass of triangle \(ABC\) lies on \(BD\) (called a median) and is 2/3 the distance from \(B\) to \(D\) (or \(1/3\) the distance from \(D\) to \(B\)). Archimedes’ proved this fact, which we will momentarily, but first let’s see where this gets us. We have
\begin{align*} \text{Area of parabolic segment }\cdot \left(DJ\right)\amp{}=\left(\text{Area of triangle }\triangle ABC\right)\cdot \left(\frac{BD}{3}\right)\\ \amp{} = \left(\text{ Area of triangle } \triangle ABC\right)\cdot \left(\frac{DJ}{3}\right)\\ \amp{}=\frac{1}{3}\text{ Area of triangle } \triangle ABC \end{align*}
which is the result we stated originally. Numerically, this says that the area of the parabolic segment is \(\frac{1}{6}a^3\text{.}\)
🔗
To see that the center of mass of a triangle lies on any median of that triangle, notice that any slice parallel to the side of a triangle is bisected by the median to that side.
🔗
This says that all of the slices of the triangle balance on the median and so the triangle itself balances on the median and the center of mass must be located on it. Thus, the center of mass will be the intersection of two medians (it in fact is the intersection of all three). Let’s apply this to triangle \(ABC\ \)above.
🔗

Definition 20.1.1.11. Parabolic Section.

A parabolic section is the region which a straight line cuts from a parabola. The shaded region in this sketch is a parabolic section.
🔗
🔗
Archimedes was able to compute the area of a parabolic section precisely using Eudoxus’ Method of Exhaustion. Because we are more interested in the underlying ideas we will not follow Archimedes’ argument in detail, but we will consider the broad outline since the method itself is fundamental and enlightening.
🔗
Archimedes began by inscribing the triangle \(\triangle ABC\) inside the sector as in Figure 20.1.1.12. Note that the point \(B\) is located horizontally halfway between points \(A\) and \(C\text{.}\)
🔗
Observing that the line segments \(\overline{AB}\) and \(\overline{BC}\) also cut off sections of the parabola, he then inscribed the triangles \(\triangle ABD\) and \(\triangle BCE\) as in Figure 20.1.1.13. Archimedes was then able to show that
\begin{equation*} \text{area of }\triangle ABD + \triangle BCE =\frac{1}{4}\text{(area of } \triangle ABC). \text{.} \end{equation*}
🔗
Since the line segments \(\overline{AD}\text{,}\) \(\overline{DB}\text{,}\) \(\overline{BE}\text{,}\) and \(\overline{EC}\) define four more parabolic sections Archimedes continued repeating this process with progressivly smaller rectangles. He was able to show (essentially) that the area of the original parabolic section is:
\begin{equation*} \triangle ABC\left[1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \right] \end{equation*}
which he was able to show is equal to
\begin{equation*} \frac{4}{3}\left(\text{Area of }\triangle ABC\right). \end{equation*}
🔗
We will return to this in [provisional cross-reference: Infinite Series] where we will develop a method for computing the sum
\begin{equation*} 1+\frac{1}{4}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^2+\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 + \cdots \end{equation*}
We will also extend our method to more general infinite sums.
