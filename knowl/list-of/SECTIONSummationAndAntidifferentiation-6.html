Differentiation, Antidifferentiation, and Integration, Oh My!

The point of this discussion is to clarify the vocabulary that we will be using.
  1. Recall that differentiation is a process. We start with a function \(y=f(x) \) and differentiate to generate the differential \(\dx{y}=f^\prime(x)\dx{x}\text{.}\) Then we divide through by \(\dx{x}\) to obtain the function we call the derivative of \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The expression \(\dx{y}=f^\prime(x)\dx{x}\) appears to represent a single differential and until now we’ve always referred to it the singular to avoid unnecessary confusion. But notice that it actually represents many differentials — one for each value of \(x\text{.}\) Thus dividing by \(\dx{x}\) yields the value of \(f(x)\) for all values of \(x\) as well. Differentiation can be seen as the process of separating the component differentials of the derivative function. Outside of mathematics this is exactly what “diffentiation” has always meant, to “separate the pieces.”
    🔗
    Until now we’ve always been careful to call first step, the act of computing the differentials, “differentiation” but in common parlance “differentiation” has come to mean the entire two step process which terminates with the derivative.
