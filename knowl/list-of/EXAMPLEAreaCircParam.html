Example 26.1.0.1. The Area of a Circle With Radius \(\boldsymbol{= a}\).

A circle with radius \(a\) can be parametrized by:
\begin{equation*} P(\theta)= \ParamEqTwo {x(\theta)} {y(\theta)}= \ParamEqTwo {a\cos(\theta))} {a\sin(\theta)} \end{equation*}
where \(0\le{}\theta{}\lt{}2\pi{}\) so the area of the circle is given by
\begin{gather*} \int{}y\dx{x} = \int{}\underbrace{a\sin\left(\theta{}\right)}_{y(\theta{})}\underbrace{\left(-a\sin(\theta)\right) \dx{\theta}}_{\dx{x}} = a^2\int{}\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}, \end{gather*}
once we find appropriate indices. Unfortunately this will not always be a simple matter. Indeed, you will find that the hardest part of the integration of parametric functions is often just finding the right indices. Let’s see why.
🔗
At first its seems that this integral is relatively simple: Simply integrate from right to left as usual. This gives
\begin{gather*} a^2\int_{x=-a}^a\sin^2(\theta)\dx{\theta } \end{gather*}
which has several problems.
🔗
Notice that the integration variable is \(\theta\) not \(x\text{.}\) To be sure we’ve seen integrals where the indices did not match the integration variable before, but we always arranged for the indices and the integration variable to match before proceeding to compute the integral. We will need for them to match here too.
🔗
One option is to re–express \(\theta{}\) in terms of \(x\text{.}\) That will work but it is an unattractive choice since it requires that invert the function \(x(\theta{})\text{.}\)
🔗
A much more attractive choice is to find \(\theta{}\) such that
\begin{gather*} x(\theta{}) = a\cos(\theta{}) = -a. \end{gather*}
Solving for \(\theta{}\) we find that \(\theta{}=\pi{}\text{.}\) Similarly when \(x(\theta{})=a\text{,}\) \(\theta{}=0\) so it appears that we need to compute
\begin{gather} a^2\int_{\theta =\pi{}}^0\sin^2(\theta )\dx{\theta }\tag{26.2} \end{gather}
but this is still inadequate since the value of this integral is zero, which is obviously wrong. Try it and see.
🔗
So what went wrong?
🔗
We began by thinking of integration as “summing up the areas of rectangles” because it is an intuitive and attractive starting point. But in much the same way that not every derivative is a slope not every integral is the sum of the areas of rectangles.
🔗
Look closely at equation (26.2). We can certainly interpret the differentials \(\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}\) as “rectangles with infinitesimal width” but will summing their areas give us the area of the circle. Are they even inside the the circle? Do you see anything that you can interpret as “differential rectangles filling the circle?” Clearly not.
🔗
This is puzzling but the problem lies in our “way of thinking” about integration. It is too narrow, too closely tied to the idea of summing the areas of rectangles. We need to expand it a bit.
