Example 26.1.0.1. The Area of a Circle With Radius \(\boldsymbol{=r}\).

A circle with radius \(r\) can be parametrized by:
\begin{gather*} (-r,0) \end{gather*}
\begin{equation*} P(\theta)= \ParamEqTwo {x(\theta)} {y(\theta)}= \ParamEqTwo {r\cos(\theta)} {r\sin(\theta)} \text{.} \end{equation*}
where \({0\le{}\theta{}\lt{}2\pi{}}\text{.}\) So the area of the circle is given by
\begin{gather*} \int{}y\dx{x} = \int{}\underbrace{r\sin\left(\theta{}\right)}_{y(\theta{})}\underbrace{\left(-r\sin(\theta)\right) \dx{\theta}}_{\dx{x}} = r^2\int{}\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}, \end{gather*}
once we find appropriate indices.
🔗
At first this integral appears to be relatively simple. After all, we’ve seen it before. But notice that we haven’t indicated where to start or stop summing our differentials. That’s because in the context of parametric functions finding the correct integration bounds is not as simple as it has been so far. Indeed, it will often be the case that finding the right integration bounds is the hardest part of the integration of parametric functions. We’ll see why that is next but before we begin make your best guess: What do you think the integration bounds should be for this problem.
🔗
Speaking naively, it appears that we need only integrate from \(-r\) to \(r\) — from left to right — just like always:
\begin{gather*} r^2\int_{x=-r}^r\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}. \end{gather*}
But notice that the integrand is in terms of \(\theta{}\) while the bounds are in terms of \(x\text{.}\) We know from our work in Section 19.1 that we can not mix the variables like this. Clearly we need to find \(x\) in terms of \(\theta{}\text{.}\)
🔗
Since \(x=r\cos(\theta{})\) we see that \(\theta{}=\pi{}\) when \(x=-r\text{.}\) Similarly \(\theta{}=0\) when \(x=r\text{.}\) So it would appear that the definite integral wee need to evaluate is
\begin{gather*} r^2\int_{\theta{}=\pi{}}^0\sin^2(\theta{})\dx{\theta{}}. \end{gather*}
🔗
But this still doesn’t work. When we evaluate this integral we will get \(\frac{1}{2}\pi{}r^2\) but it has been known since ancient times that the area of circle with radius \(r\) is \(\pi{}r^2\text{.}\) As we proceed through the computation see if you can identify what goes wrong.
🔗
By the Half-Angle formula \(\cos^2(\theta{}) = \frac12 \left( 1+\cos(2\theta{})\right)\) we have
\begin{align*} r^2\int_{\theta{}=\pi{}}^0\sin^2(\theta)\dx{\theta}\amp{}=r^2\int_{\theta{}=\pi{}}^0\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\cos(2\theta{})\right)\dx{\theta{}}\\ \amp{}= \frac{r^2}{2}\left[1-\cos(2\theta{})\right]_{\theta{}=\pi{}}^0\\ \amp{}=\frac{1}{2}\pi{}r^2. \end{align*}
🔗
Did you spot the error. Take a moment before you continue reading to consider. It is quite subtle.
🔗

Using this diagram we explicitly encouraged you to think of an integral — especially an integral which represents an area — as the sum of infinitely small rectangles. We have always drawn our sketches so that we start summing at the left and end on the right, but there was no compelling reason to emphasize that fact since we can sum rectangles in any order whatsoever. There is no directionality.
