Example 4.1.0.2. The Buffon Needle Problem.

For example, in this example we will convert an abstract problem — the computation of a probability — into a concrete, visible problem in geometry: compute the ratio of two areas. Such a conversion is helpful because it allows us to bring our visual intuition into play. The upshot of this discussion is that you will soon be asked to hone your skills by computing lots of areas and volumes of various shapes, more or less in the absence of any compelling need. Understand that this is simply practice. When you encounter problems which reduce to computing an area or volume these you’ll want to be able to do this last step easily.
🔗
In 1777 Georges Louis Leclerc, Le Comte de Buffon (the Count of Buffon), presented to the Royal Academy of Science in Paris what has since become known as the “Buffon Needle Problem.”
🔗
Suppose that a needle is dropped randomly onto the floor where a series of parallel grid lines are drawn at a distance of one needle apart, as in the sketch below.
🔗
Leclerc had determined that the probability that a needle will land crossing on of the grid lines is exactly \(\frac{2}{\pi }\) which may seem a little counter-intuitive. How does \(\pi \) show up in a problem without a circle in it?
🔗
To keep things simple we will assume that the length of a needle is \(1\) (so we are measuring in “needle lengths” rather than inches, or centimeters, or light-years) and we’ll focus our attention on a single throw of the needle as depicted in Figure 4.1.0.6.
🔗
A few facts are clear from Figure 4.1.0.5:
Fact 1:
🔗
A throw is specified by the angle \((\theta )\) and the distance \((\delta )\) from the lower end of the needle to the next grid line. That is, each ordered pair \(\left(\theta , \delta \right)\) represents one thrown needle.
🔗
Fact 2:
🔗
A needle will cross a grid line only when \(\delta \le\sin\left(\theta \right) \text{.}\)
🔗
Fact 3:
🔗
\(\displaystyle 0 \le\delta \le1 \)
🔗
Fact 4:
🔗
\(\displaystyle 0\le \theta \le \pi \)
🔗
🔗
Thus each point \((\theta, \delta )\) in the \(\pi \times\delta \) rectangle (as seen in Figure 4.1.0.6 below) represents a single throw of the needle.
🔗
If you throw a dart randomly at a rectangle the probability of hitting the right half of the rectangle is \(\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\) of hitting the left one-third is \(\frac{1}{3}\text{,}\) of hitting the center two-fifths is \(\frac{2}{5}\text{,}\) and so on. This idea called geometric probability. In general, if you have a planar region \(R\text{,}\) whose area is \(A_1\) and a sub-region \(S\text{,}\) whose area is \(A_2\) the probabilty that a randomly chosen point in \(R\) will also be in the sub-region is the ratio: \(\frac{A_2}{A_1}\text{.}\)
🔗
The area of the entire rectangle in Figure 4.1.0.5 is clearly \(\pi \) (why?), so we can solve Buffon’s problem if we can determine the area under the graph of \(\delta =\sin\left(\theta \right)\) between \(0\) and \(\pi \text{.}\)
🔗
But this is straightforward. The area under \(\delta =\sin\left(\theta \right)\) will be the value of the definite integral
\begin{equation*} \int_{0}^{\pi }\sin\left(\theta \right)\dx{\theta } \left(=2 \text{ if Buffon was correct}\right). \end{equation*}
🔗
But why guess when we can compute? We see that
\begin{align*} \int_{0}^{\pi }\sin\left(\theta \right)\dx{\theta } \amp{}= \left[\int\sin\left(\theta\right)\dx{\theta } \right]_{\theta =0}^{\pi}\\ \amp{}= \tbeval{-\cos\left(\theta \right)}{\theta =0}{\pi }\\ \amp{}=-\left[\cos\left(\pi \right)\right] - \left[-\cos\left(0\right)\right]\\ \amp{}=2. \end{align*}
Thus the probability of a Buffon needle landing on a gridline is \(\frac{2}{\pi }\) just as Leclerc claimed.
