Problem 20.3.0.1. Integrals Are Not Areas.

For example consider the definite integral
\begin{gather*} \int_{x=-2}^{2}x\dx{x} \end{gather*}
Evaluating we have
\begin{gather*} \tbeval{\frac{x^2}{2}}{x=-2}{2}=\frac{2^2}{2}-\frac{(-2)^2}{2}=0 \end{gather*}
Since areas are—by definition—positive this is clearly not an area. To see what’s going on here we’ll sketch the problem.
🔗
It should be clear that each differential rectangle below the \(x\)-axis (to the left of the \(y\)-axis) will have a corresponding rectangle above the \(x\)-axis (to the right of the \(y\)-axis). Both will have a width of \(\dx{x}\) and a height of \(y(x)\) so both have the same area. The area on the right is given by \(y\dx{x}\) since both \(y\) and \(\dx{x}\) are positive. However on the left the quantity \(y\dx{x}\) is negative because at that point \(y(x)\lt0 \) and \(\dx{x} \gt0\text{.}\) So \(y(x)\dx{x} \) cannot be an area.
🔗
The rectangles on the left and right of the \(y\)-axis do have the same area. The area of the rectangles on the right is given by \(y(x)\dx{x} \) but on the left they will be \(\abs{y(x)}\dx{x}\) because on the left \(y(x)\dx{x}\lt 0\) and areas are positive by definition.
🔗
An integral computes the sum of the differentials of the form \(y(x)\dx{x} \text{,}\) not \(\abs{y(x)}\dx{x} \text{,}\) and in this problem for every differential on the right side of the graph there is a corresponding differential on the left side with the same magnitude but opposite sign. Thus they will all sum to zero. Which is not an area.
🔗
It is common—and wrong— to say that the rectangles on the left have “negative area” so they “cancel” the rectangles on the right. Be careful. Thinking this way is deceptive. It works just often enough to lead you into carelessness. Avoid it. There is no such thing as a “negative area.”
🔗
