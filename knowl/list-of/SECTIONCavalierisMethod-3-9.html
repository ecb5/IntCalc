Example 20.1.2.3.

Consider the region \(R\) bounded by the parabola \({y=x^2} \text{,}\) and the lines \(y=0\) and \(x=a\) as in the diagram in Figure 20.1.2.4 above. We will use Archimedes’ Method and to show that the area of \(R\) is \(2/3\) of the area of \(\triangle ABC\text{.}\)
🔗
First we construct a vertical line at an arbitrary location, \(x\text{.}\) This creates two line segments: \(EF\) in the interior of \(\triangle ABC\text{,}\) and \(GF\) in the interior of the region \(R\) as seen in the upper diagram in Figure 20.1.2.5. Next imagine the \(x\)-axis is a balance beam with its fulcrum at the origin as seen in the lower diagram in Figure 20.1.2.5.
🔗
In Problem 20.1.2.7 below we ask you to show that the two segments will balance if we leave the line segment \(EF\) in place and move line segment \(GF\) to the point \((-a,0)\text{.}\) If this is so then according to Archimedes if the region \(R\) is placed \((-a,0) \) it will balance with triangle \(ABC\) left in its place as in Figure 20.1.2.6.
🔗
The difficulty now is that we have \(R\) located at a single point on our balance, but \(\triangle ABC\) lies above the entire line segment from \((0,0 \) to \((a,0)\) so we can’t yet apply the Law of the Lever. Fortunately it is known that the weight of \(\triangle ABC\) will act as if it is a point mass located at its center of mass. Archimedes also showed that the center of mass of will lie at the common intersection of the three lines from each vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. For \(\triangle ABC\) this is the point \(\left(\frac{2a}{3},\frac{a}{3}\right) \text{.}\) Thus according to Archimedes’ Law of the Lever we see that
\begin{equation*} \left(\text{area of }R\right)\cdot a=\left(\text{area of } \triangle ABC\right)\cdot \frac{2}{3}a \text{.} \end{equation*}
