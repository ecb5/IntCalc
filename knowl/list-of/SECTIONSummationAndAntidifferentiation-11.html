Integral vs. Antiderivative.

It may appear that we have taken a lot of effort to do very little. First we solved equation (18.2) by simply reversing the Power Rule to get
\begin{gather*} y(x)=x^3. \end{gather*}
We called this solution the antiderivative of \(3x^2\dx{x}\text{.}\)
🔗
Then we solved it again (or at least we tried to) by summing the differentials on either side of equation (18.2): \(y(x) = \int3x^2\dx{x}\text{.}\) However, since we have no way to compute this sum we simply fell back on the knowledge that \(y(x) = x^3\) is the antiderivative of \(3x^2\) to obtain the integral
\begin{gather*} y(x) = \int3x^2\dx{x}=x^3. \end{gather*}
It would appear that integral and antiderivative mean the same thing and you are entirely justified if you are feeling a little frustrated that we have taken so long, and so much effort to say so.
🔗
Let us explain. In fact in this simple case integral and antiderivative are the same thing. But they are distinct objects and the difference will be important in the future. The distintion is this: An antiderivative is a function derived from a function whereas an integral is the result of summing differentials. In some cases (like this one) an integral will also be a function but not always.
🔗
That the integral \(\int3x^2\dx{x}\) is equal to the antiderivative of \(3x^2\) is a simple example of what has been named

🔗
It is important to get this clear in your mind early on because the notation that comes with integration, and with integrals of various kinds, will be very confusing otherwise. This is succintly stated with the mantra, “We Integrate Differentials, Not Functions.”
