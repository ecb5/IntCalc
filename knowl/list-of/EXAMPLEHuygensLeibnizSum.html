Example 1.1.0.3. The Problem Huygens Posed to Leibniz.

When Leibniz was living in Paris he asked Christiaan Huygens to tutor him in the latest developments in mathematics. Huygens was a famous and respected elder scientist at the time, whereas Leibniz was a young man in his twenties and completely unknown. To evaluate Leibniz’ abilities Hugens posed a problem very similar to this one, but with many more terms.
🔗
Leibniz quickly realized that the terms of the sum could be re-expressed as
\begin{equation*} \underbrace{\frac{1}{1\cdot{}2 }}_{=\frac{1}{2}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\cdot{}3 }}_{=\frac{1}{6}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{3\cdot{4}}}_{=\frac{1}{12}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{4\cdot{5}}}_{=\frac{1}{20}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{5\cdot{6}}}_{\frac{1}{30}}. \end{equation*}
He then observed that in general
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{n(n+1) }=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1} \end{equation*}
so the problem could be re-written as
\begin{equation*} \left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{5}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{5}-\frac{1}{6}\right). \end{equation*}
which sums to
\begin{equation*} 1-\frac{1}{6}=\frac{5}{6} \end{equation*}
since all of the other terms add to zero.
🔗
it is easy to understand how this can be if we think about finite differences (rather than infinitesimal difference) instead.
