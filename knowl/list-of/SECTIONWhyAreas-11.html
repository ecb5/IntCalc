In Section 19.3 we presented the technique of integration by parts as the integral operation associated with the product rule. We also mentioned that Leibniz called his version of this the Transmutation Theorem because it replaced one integral (presumably not readily integrated) with another which hopefully is more manageable. Leibniz’ derivation did not involve the product rule as his transmutation theorem preceded his differentiation rules and he used it to compute areas under curves (known as a quadrature problem). As such, his derivation was geometric and his idea was to determine the area of a region below a curve by introducing a curve whose region was easier to determine. His “transmuted” curve was generated using the tangent to the existing curve. Here is Leibniz’ geometric idea. To simplify matters a bit, suppose we want to compute the area of the following triangular region \(OAB\) bounded above by the green curve, below by the horizontal line segment \(OA\) and the vertical line segment \(AB\text{.}\)
🔗
Leibniz showed that this area is equal to the area of triangle \(OAB\) plus \(\frac{1}{2}\) of the area under the following transmuted blue curve.
🔗
Of course, the transmuted curve is not just any curve; it is constructed in a very particular way involving the tangent to the green curve. With that in mind, choose any point \(X\) on the green curve and draw the tangent line to that curve hitting the vertical line through \(O\) at \(C\text{.}\) The point on the blue curve is where the horizontal line through \(C\) intersects the vertical line through \(X\text{.}\) The following picture conveys this better than the words.
🔗
To see that this curve does what Leibniz says it does, let’s put in coordinate axes \(v\) and \(u\) (you’ll see why we made this odd choice for coordinates soon) and parameterize the green curve by \(\ParamEqThree{u = u(t)}{v = v(t)} {t_{0} \leq t \leq t_{1}} \) with
\begin{equation*} O = (0,0) = \left( v\left( t_{0} \right),u\left( t_{0} \right) \right) \end{equation*}
and
\begin{equation*} B = \left( v\left( t_{1} \right),u\left( t_{1} \right) \right)\text{.} \end{equation*}
Let \(X = (v,u) = \left( v(t),u(t) \right)\) represent the generic point on the green curve. Finally, let the length of \(OC\) be given by \(z\) so that the point on the blue curve has coordinates \((v,z)\) With this labeling, the area of region \(OABX\) is given by
\begin{equation*} \int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{u\dx{v}} \end{equation*}
🔗
It is also the sum of the area of triangle \(OAB\) and the region bounded by the green curve and the line segment \(OB\text{.}\)
🔗
Let’s focus on that region. If we let \(h\) denote the length of the perpendicular to the tangent line through \(O\text{,}\) then the area of the triangle with base \(\dx{s}\) and vertex \(O\) has area \(\frac{1}{2}h\dx{s}\text{.}\) See below.
🔗
Leibniz surmised that the area of the region bounded by the green curve and line segment \(OB\) is the sum (integral) of the areas of these infinitely thin triangles. In our integral notation, this is
🔗
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2}\int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{h\dx{s}} \end{equation*}
🔗
Let’s now put \(z\) and a differential triangle at \(X\) back into the picture.
🔗

Problem 21.1.0.1.

Show that \(\dfdx{v}{s} = \frac{h}{z}\) and use this to show that
🔗
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2}\int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_1}{h\dx{s}} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_1}{z\dx{v}} \end{equation*}
🔗
🔗
Problem 21.1.0.1 says that the area of the original region \(OABX\) is the sum of the area of triangle \(OAB\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\) the area under the curve \(\left( v(t),z(t) \right)\) [the blue curve]. This is Leibniz transmutation theorem. In our modern integral notation this is given by
🔗
\begin{equation*} \int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{u\dx{v}} = \frac{1}{2}u\left( t_{1} \right)v\left( t_{1} \right) + \frac{1}{2}\int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{z\dx{v}} \end{equation*}
🔗
To get the integration by parts formula out of this, we need to get \(z\) in terms of \(v\) and \(u\text{.}\)
🔗

Problem 21.1.0.2.

Consider the following diagram
🔗

(a)

Show that \(\dfdx{u}{v} = \frac{u - z}{v}\) and use this to show that
\begin{equation*} z\dx{v} = u\dx{v} - v\dx{u} \end{equation*}
🔗
🔗

(b)

Substitute the result from part (a) into
\begin{equation*} \int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{u\dx{v}} = \frac{1}{2}u\left( t_{1} \right)v\left( t_{1} \right) + \frac{1}{2}\int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{z\dx{v}} \end{equation*}
to obtain
\begin{equation*} \int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{u\dx{v}} = \ftceval{u(t)v(t)}{t}{t_0}{t_1} - \int_{t = t_{0}}^{t_{1}}{v\dx{u}} \end{equation*}
which is the integration by parts formula applied to this definite integral.
