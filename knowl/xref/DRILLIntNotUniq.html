Drill 18.1.0.1.

Notice that we called it an antiderivative, not the antiderivative. What follows will be complicated by the fact that if a function has an antiderivative then it must have more than one. We will ignore this complication for now.
🔗
Identify three antiderivatives of \(3x^2\) which are different from \(x^3\) and from each other. Are there others? How many?
🔗
Recall that finding a derivative was a two-step process: we had to differentiate using our rules of differentiation and then divide by a differential such as \(dx,\ dt,\) etc. To find an antiderivative, we must reverse this process: we must multiply by a differential and then apply the correct antidifferentiation rule. For example, if we had
\begin{equation*} \frac{dy}{dx}=3x^2 \end{equation*}
then we multiply by \(dx\) to get
\begin{equation*} dy=3x^2dx \end{equation*}
Utilizing the integration notation we introduced before, we have
\begin{equation*} \int{dy}=\int{3x^2dx} \end{equation*}
Of course, this is a bit murky as we already noted that antiderivatives are not unique. Clearly, we need to examine this more in depth. The first order of business is to unravel the notation and terminology.
