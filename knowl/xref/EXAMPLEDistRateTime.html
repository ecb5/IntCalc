Example 21.1.0.1. Some Simple Examples.

(a) Distance=Rate\(\boldsymbol{\times}\)Time.

As a simple example consider the following. Suppose you are traveling from your home to another town at a rate. If you drive at \(r_1 \frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}\text{,}\) for \(t_1\) hours, then the distance \(D\text{,}\) that you travel will be
\begin{equation} D = r_1\times{t_1}.\tag{21.1} \end{equation}
You should be familiar with this from your previous studies.
🔗
This problem can be recast as an area problem by letting \(t\) and \(r\) be represented by horizontal and vertical axes and graphing equation (21.1) in that coordinate system. In that case the distance traveled, \(D\text{,}\) is given by the area of the shaded region in the following sketch.
🔗
You can confirm that the shaded region represents distance traveled by checking the units involved. Since \(t_1\) represents time it is measured in one of the units of time, say hours. Since \(r_1\) represents a velocity it is measured in say, \(\frac{\text{miles}}{\text{hour}}\text{.}\) Thus we see that \(D=r_1\times t_1\) is measured in \(\frac{\text{miles}}{\cancel{\text{hour}}}\times\cancel{\text{hours}}=\text{miles} \text{.}\)
🔗
🔗

(b)

Of course the model in part (a) is hopelessly unrealistic for a couple of reasons. For one, if we were to instanteously accelerate from \(0 \frac{miles}{\text{hour}}\) to \(r_1 \frac{miles}{\text{hour}}\) at the beginning or decelerate instanteously back to zero at the end we would die if \(r_1\) is any reasonable rate of speed. Moreover it is not possible to move at a constant rate of speed. Velocity will always vary. We can create a (very slightly) more realistic to take model by taking the velocity to be
\begin{equation*} v(t) = t(t_1-t). \end{equation*}
This will ensure that our velocity is zero at the beginnning and end of the trip so we don’t die when we start or stop.
🔗
The distance \(\dx{D}\) traveled during a moment \(\dx{t}\) will be: \(\dx{D}=v(t)\dx{t}\text{.}\) Adding all of these together we see that the total distance traveled is given by
\begin{align*} D=\int_{t=0}^{t_1}\dx{D} \amp{}=\int_{t=0}^{t_1}v(t)\dx{t}\\ \amp{} =\int_{t=0}^{t_1}t(t_1-t)\dx{t}\\ \amp{}=\eval{\left(\frac{t^2}{2}t_1-\frac{t^3}{3}\right)}{t}{0}^{t_1}\\ \amp{}=\frac{t_1^3}{6}. \end{align*}
🔗
Just as in part (a) you can verify that the result of the integration yields the correct physical value (distance in this case) by checking the units: \(v(t)\) is measured as \(\frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}\) and \(\dx{t}\) measures time to their product yields distance.
🔗
🔗

(c)

An amusement park tracks of the number of guests entering the park each day by measuring the rate at which guests enter through the gates. If we let \(G_r(t))\) represent the rate of entry (measured in guests per minute) the graph in Figure 21.1.0.2 is typical.

Clearly the area beneath the curve between 8AM and 8PM represents the total number of guests who entered the park that day. That area is given by the integral:
\begin{equation*} \int_{t=8\text{AM}}^{8\text{PM}}G_r(t))\dx{t}. \end{equation*}
